:::scene{type=title}
narration: |
  Welcome to lesson three point two, code as a design medium. This is the lesson where
  code stops being a calculator and becomes a way of designing. You will describe a
  building as a rule, generate a whole family of variants in one loop, and see a
  trade off appear as a curve. Then you will learn how to write a brief that gets an
  assistant to build tools like that for you.
:::

:::scene{type=compare}
heading: A drawing or a program?
left:
  title: A drawing
  points:
    - Shows one building
    - You can look at it
    - You cannot ask it anything
right:
  title: A program
  points:
    - Describes a family of buildings
    - A number for each member
    - You can sweep it, sort it, plot it
verdict: The design is the rule, not the drawing. The drawing is one output among many.
narration: |
  A drawing shows one building. A program describes a family of buildings: every
  width, depth, floor count and glazing ratio the rule allows, and a number for each of
  them. When the design lives in code, changing your mind costs one keystroke, a
  hundred variants cost one loop, and the reasons for a choice are written down where
  a reviewer can run them. That is what code as a design medium means. Not that
  architects become programmers, but that a rule you can execute is a different kind of
  design object from a picture you can only look at.
:::

:::scene{type=cards}
heading: Four words
items:
  - title: Parameter

    tag: input
  - title: Rule

  - title: Metric

narration: |
  Four words carry the lesson. A parameter is a number you choose: width, depth,
  floors, window to wall ratio. A rule is how the parameters become a building and its
  numbers: the function. A variant is one setting of the parameters, one call of the
  function. And a metric is a number that says something about a variant: floor area,
  compactness, a daylight proxy. Grasshopper and Dynamo are this idea with wires. Python
  is the same idea with less to click and more to read.
:::

:::scene{type=code}
heading: A bar building as a rule
code: |
  def bar(width, depth, floors, wwr, f2f=3.6, reach=6.0):
      plate  = width * depth
      walls  = 2 * (width + depth) * floors * f2f
      inner  = max(0, width - 2*reach) * max(0, depth - 2*reach)
      return {"floor_area_m2":   plate * floors,
              "glazing_m2":      round(walls * wwr, 1),
              "s_over_v":        round((walls + plate) / (plate * floors * f2f), 3),
              "daylit_fraction": round(1 - inner / plate, 3)}

  print(bar(40, 16, 3, 0.35))
output: |
  {'floor_area_m2': 1920, 'glazing_m2': 423.4,
   's_over_v': 0.268, 'daylit_fraction': 0.825}
note: Four parameters in, four numbers out. The daylit fraction is the share of the floor plate within six metres of a façade — a rule of thumb, not a simulation.
narration: |
  Here is the smallest useful design tool. A rectangular bar building, described by
  four parameters: width, depth, floors, and window to wall ratio. The rule computes
  the floor plate, the wall area, and the part of the plate that is more than six
  metres from any facade. Out come four numbers: floor area, glazing area, surface to
  volume ratio, which is a compactness measure, and a daylit fraction, the share of the
  floor within six metres of a window. Six metres is a rule of thumb, not a simulation,
  and we will come back to that.
:::

narration: |
  Now the moment code becomes a design medium. One loop calls the rule sixteen times,
  for depths from ten metres to forty, with everything else fixed. Plot the results and
  the trade off the whole family contains appears as two curves. Deeper plates are more
  compact: the surface to volume ratio falls. And deeper plates are less daylit: the
  daylit fraction falls too. No drawing could show you this. The two curves put a
  number on what each metre of depth buys and costs.
:::

:::scene{type=cards}
heading: A brief that leaves nothing to guess
items:
  - title: Purpose
    text: One sentence. What the tool is for, who reads its output.
  - title: Inputs, with units
    text: Every parameter, its unit, a sensible range. Width and depth in metres, 8 to 80.
  - title: Outputs, with units
    text: Every number it returns and what it means.
  - title: One worked example
    text: 40 × 16 m, 3 floors, WWR 0.35 gives floor area 1920 m² and daylit fraction 0.825.
narration: |
  The rule you just saw is exactly the kind of thing an assistant writes well, if the
  brief is precise. A brief that leaves nothing to guess has five parts. The purpose, in
  one sentence. Every input with its unit and range. Every output with its unit. One
  worked example with the numbers you expect. And the constraints: no libraries except
  the ones you allow, no input prompts. Compare that with the one sentence brief that
  produced the bugs in lesson three point one.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=check}
text: The worked example is the test.
sub: If you cannot write the worked example, you are not ready to ask for the tool — and finding that out is useful.
narration: |
  The worked example is the test. When the brief contains a case with its expected
  answer, the assistant has a target, and you have an assertion to paste under the
  code. Forty by sixteen, three floors, gives a daylit fraction of zero point eight two
  five: if the code says otherwise, you know before you read a line. And if you cannot
  write the worked example, you are not ready to ask for the tool. Finding that out is
  useful.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=warn}
text: Code makes the design space visible. / It does not judge it.
sub: A daylit fraction from a six-metre rule is a proxy. Modules 5 to 8 replace proxies with measured and simulated evidence.
narration: |
  Last idea. Code makes the design space visible. It does not judge it. The daylit
  fraction from a six metre rule is a proxy: it tells you quickly that a twenty eight
  metre deep plate has a dark middle. It does not tell you the daylight in any room,
  which depends on orientation, glazing, obstructions, the sky and the schedule. Modules
  five to eight are about replacing proxies with measured and simulated evidence. The
  design tool's job is earlier and humbler: make the whole family visible so the
  expensive evaluation is spent on the right few members.
:::
