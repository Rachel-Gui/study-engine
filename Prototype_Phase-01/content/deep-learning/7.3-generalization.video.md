:::scene{type=title}
narration: |
  Welcome to lesson seven point three, generalization, overfitting and reliable
  evaluation. In lesson six point five you learned the rule: a model is worth what it
  does on data it has never seen. Now you have a network with tens of thousands of
  weights, and it can memorise a small dataset the way you memorise a phone number. This
  lesson is about noticing when it does, three cheap ways to stop it, and the split that
  makes the number you report a valid one.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=question}
text: Why can a model be nearly perfect on its training data / and fail on a new building?
sub: Because fitting the training rows and learning the pattern behind them are different jobs, and a big enough network can do the first without the second.
narration: |
  The question: why can a model perform extremely well on training data and still fail
  on a new building, or a new climate? Because fitting the training rows and learning
  the pattern behind them are two different jobs. A big enough network can do the first
  without doing the second at all. It simply memorises. And nothing in the training score
  tells you which one happened.
:::

:::scene{type=diagram id=fit_regimes}
heading: The same twelve points, three model capacities
points:
  - Underfit — a line through a curve; both errors high
  - Good fit — follows the pattern, not the noise
  - Overfit — hits every point, misses the next one
narration: |
  Here are the same twelve points, fitted three ways. On the left, a model that is too
  simple: a line through a curve, wrong everywhere, both errors high. In the middle, a
  model that follows the pattern but not the noise. On the right, a model that hits
  every single point, and will miss the next one, because it has learned the noise as if
  it were signal. The third one has the best training score of the three. That is the
  whole problem.
:::

:::scene{type=diagram id=overfitting}
heading: Training loss always falls. Validation loss tells the truth.
points:
  - Training curve — always improving
  - Validation curve — improves, stalls, turns upward
  - "Stop here — the bottom of the validation curve"
  - "After the turn — memorising, not learning"
narration: |
  How you notice it. Plot two curves as training runs. The training loss always falls;
  the network can always fit its own rows a little better. The validation loss, on rows
  the network never trains on, falls too, then stalls, then turns upward. Everything
  after the turn is memorising, not learning. So the recipe is: split first, train on one
  part, watch the other, stop at the bottom, and report the third part once.
:::

  A check. A network scores R squared zero point nine nine on its training rows and zero

:::

:::scene{type=cards}
heading: Regularization — ways to stop a network memorising
items:
  - title: Early stopping
    text: Stop when validation stops improving, and keep the best weights. Free, and always on.
    tag: default
  - title: Dropout
    text: Randomly switch off some neurons during training, so the network cannot depend on any one of them.
    tag: default
  - title: Weight decay
    text: Discourage unnecessarily large weights. Large weights are how a network fits single points.
    tag: cheap
  - title: More data, or augmentation
    text: Meaningful variations that do not change the target. The fix that always works, when you can afford it.
    tag: best
narration: |
  Regularization is the name for anything that makes memorising harder than learning.
  Early stopping: stop when the validation curve turns, and keep the best weights. Free,
  and it should always be on. Dropout: during training, randomly switch off some neurons
  on every step, so the network cannot lean on any single one; a dropout of zero point
  two means one neuron in five. Weight decay: penalise large weights, because large
  weights are how a network bends itself around a single point. And more data, or
  meaningful variations of the data you have, which is the fix that always works when
  you can afford it. Dropout and early stopping are your defaults, not your last resort.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=warn}
text: A negative R² in the leakage step / is not a bug.
sub: The network learned "look at the neighbouring rows". Split by time, and the neighbouring rows are gone. Worse than predicting the mean is what that model actually is.
narration: |
  The lab on the lesson page ends with a trap. A network trained on sensor data with a
  random split scores well. Split the same rows by time instead, and the R squared goes
  negative: worse than predicting the mean. That is not a bug in the lab. The network
  had learned to look at the neighbouring rows, which were ten minutes away and nearly
  identical. Split by time and the neighbours are gone, and what is left is what the
  model actually understands, which is very little.
:::

:::scene{type=compare}
heading: Split by the question you are asking
left:
  title: Random split
  points:
    - Shuffle, hold out 20%
    - Answers — can it predict rows like the ones it trained on?
    - For time series or repeated geometry, usually a leak
right:
  title: Grouped split
  points:
    - By building, by geometry, by time block
    - Answers — will it work on something new?
    - Lower scores — the ones that generalise. Report the grouping with the number.
verdict: The split is not a technicality. It decides which question your score answers.
narration: |
  So the split is not a technicality; it decides which question your score answers. A
  random split asks: can the model predict rows like the ones it trained on? For time
  series or repeated geometry, that is usually a leak. A grouped split, by building, by
  geometry or by time block, asks: will it work on something new? The scores are lower.
  They are the ones that hold up on new buildings. Report the grouping rule next to the number, every time, so a reader
  knows which question was answered.
:::

:::scene{type=list}
heading: The generalization tests that matter in practice
items:
  - New buildings from the same dataset — the easiest question
  - A building type absent from training
  - A different city or climate zone
  - A different geometric scale or topology
  - Extreme weather, or noisy and incomplete sensor data
narration: |
  Report a single held out score and you have answered the easiest possible question:
  new buildings from the same dataset. These are the questions a practitioner actually
  has. A building type that was absent from training. A different city or climate zone.
  A different geometric scale or topology. Extreme weather, or sensors that are noisy or
  missing. You will not be able to test all of them. Say which ones you tested, and
  which ones you did not.
:::

  - Whether the learning rate could be lowered to reach 0.95

  of a simulation dataset. What should you check first? A: nothing, zero point nine one
  is a strong result. B: whether repeated runs of the same geometry landed in both the
