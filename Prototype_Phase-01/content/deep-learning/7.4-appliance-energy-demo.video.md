:::scene{type=code}
heading: Feature engineering — wrap the clock around a circle
code: |
  # the hour as a plain number: 23 and 0 look 23 units apart. They are neighbours.
  df["hour_sin"]   = np.sin(2 * np.pi * df.date.dt.hour / 24)
  df["hour_cos"]   = np.cos(2 * np.pi * df.date.dt.hour / 24)
  df["is_weekend"] = (df.date.dt.dayofweek >= 5).astype(int)

  features = sensors + ["hour_sin", "hour_cos", "is_weekend"]
note: Occupancy drives appliance use, and occupancy follows the clock. Sine and cosine put 11 p.m. next to midnight. The same trick works for months, seasons and compass bearings.
narration: |
  Appliance use follows people, and people follow the clock, so we hand the model the
  time of day. One detail matters. If you feed in the hour as a number from zero to
  twenty three, the model thinks eleven p m and midnight are twenty three units apart,
  when they are adjacent. So wrap the hour around a circle with a sine and a cosine. Now
  midnight sits next to eleven p m, as it should. Add a weekend flag, and the features
  are ready. The same trick works for months, seasons, and compass bearings.
:::

:::scene{type=diagram id=scaling}
heading: Scaling is part of the model
points:
  - Humidity 20 to 90, pressure around 750 — wildly different scales
  - One learning rate for every weight, so the big features dominate the step
  - Standardise — subtract the mean, divide by the spread
  - Fit the scaler on the training set only
narration: |
  Now the step students skip most, and the one that costs the most. The inputs sit on
  wildly different scales: humidity from twenty to ninety percent, pressure around seven
  hundred and fifty millimetres of mercury. Gradient descent applies one learning rate to
  every weight, so the big valued features dominate every step and the small ones barely
  move. Standardise them, subtract the mean and divide by the spread, and every feature
  starts on equal terms. Fit that scaler on the training set only, and apply the same
  numbers to validation and test.
:::

:::scene{type=quiz}
question: You standardise the features by fitting the scaler on the whole dataset, then split into training and test. What have you done?
options:
  - Nothing wrong — scaling is just arithmetic
  - Leaked the test set — its mean and spread are now inside the training pipeline, and the score is flattered
  - Made the model worse, because the scaler saw more variety
answer: 1
pause: 5
explain: The scaler's mean and spread are statistics computed from the test rows. Fitting on everything hands those statistics to training. It is a small leak, and it is the habit that leads to bigger ones. Fit on train, apply to the rest.

:::scene{type=idea icon=eye}
text: Which inputs is the model actually using? / Shuffle one column and see how much the score falls.
sub: Permutation importance. And the reveal — rv1 and rv2, the planted random columns, should rank near the bottom. If a random column ranks near the top, your pipeline is broken.
narration: |
  Which inputs is the model relying on? Permutation importance answers it without
  opening the network: shuffle one column at a time, and measure how much the score
  falls. If shuffling a column barely hurts, the model was not using it. And now the
  reveal. Remember r v one and r v two, the planted random numbers? A trustworthy model
  should rank them near the bottom. Check where yours put them. If a random column ever
  ranks near the top of your importance chart, stop, and go and find what is broken.
:::

:::scene{type=compare}
heading: The real test — split by time
left:
  title: Random split
  points:
    - Shuffle, hold out 20 percent
    - Every test row has a near-twin ten minutes away, in training
    - The model scores well by half-remembering
right:
  title: Chronological split
  points:
    - Train on January to April, predict May
    - No twins. The future is really unseen
    - The score collapses
verdict: Which number would you put in front of a building owner?
narration: |
  The most important idea in the lesson. The eighty twenty split was random, and these
  rows are ten minutes apart. Almost every test row has a nearly identical twin in the
  training set, so the model can score well by half remembering rather than by
  understanding. So split by time instead: train on January through April, predict May.
  The score collapses. That is not a bug. That is the truth about how this model would
  perform if you deployed it in the house tomorrow. Which number would you put in front
  of a building owner?
:::

:::scene{type=list}
heading: What this demo establishes, and what it does not
items:
  - One house, one climate, one heating season — no cooling season in the window
  - R² and MAE on a held-out split — no uncertainty interval
  - Fails on another building, another climate, another year, or a summer load
  - A random split flatters the score — the lab measures by how much
  - Every row was treated as if it had no past — Lesson 8.3 fixes that
narration: |
  The boundary, because a number without a range is not a result. One house, one
  temperate climate, one heating dominated season, no cooling season in the window. R
  squared and M A E on a held out split, with no uncertainty interval. A model fitted
  here does not transfer to another building, another climate, another year, or a summer
  cooling load. A random split flatters the score, and the lab measures by how much. And
  every row was treated as if it had no past. Lesson eight point three gives the model
  the previous readings, and a single lag column beats all twenty eight sensors.
:::
