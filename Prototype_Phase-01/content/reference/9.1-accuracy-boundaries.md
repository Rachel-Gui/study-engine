## Accuracy boundaries

Use this checklist when reading or reporting an AI result. It covers common
interpretation errors and the information needed to assess a claim.

:::narration
Keep this list next to you whenever you read or report an A I result. It is not a
compliance checkbox. Every item on it is a mistake that is easy to make, common in
published work, and hard to undo once a reader has believed it. The last one is the
one people skip most: always state the range. A number with no stated range is not a
result.
:::

- Do not describe neural networks as systems that literally think like a human brain.
- Do not interpret correlation as causation.
- Do not interpret a p-value as the probability that a hypothesis is true, or as
  evidence of practical importance.
- Do not interpret high R² or accuracy as sufficient evidence of reliability.
- Do not present deeper networks as automatically more accurate.
- Do not use {{test set}} data for hyperparameter tuning.
- Do not allow records from the same building or simulation family to leak across
  training and test sets.
- Do not describe a graph, hypergraph, adjacency algorithm, or self-organizing map as
  a {{GNN}} unless a neural message-passing model is used.
- Do not use physics-informed ML, {{PINN}}, hybrid model, {{surrogate model}}, and
  {{INR}} as interchangeable terms.
- Do not imply that a fast surrogate always replaces high-fidelity simulation.
- Do not treat a realistic visual output as evidence of constructability or
  performance.
- **Always state** the data range, geometry range, climate range, metrics,
  {{uncertainty}}, and known failure conditions.
- When quoting a figure from a review — "68.94 % of studies", "92 % of
  practitioners" — quote its scope with it: the corpus, the years, the field, and
  which paper counted. A percentage without its denominator is not a finding.
- When you use another author's figure or diagram in your own work, cite it and
  follow its licence: openly licensed (CC BY) material may be reused with
  attribution; anything else is redrawn in your own words and credited ("after …").

:::keyidea
Before you present or submit, check each external figure, number, citation and
licence you use. A claim attributed to a paper you have not read is a claim you do
not own — the [Selected readings](readings.html) page lists the papers this course
draws on, with how to get each one.
:::

## Where the course demonstrates them

The two rules that are easiest to state and hardest to keep are demonstrated rather
than asserted:

:::compare
"High R² is not reliability" | Episode 7.4 | The same model scores R² 0.380 on a random split of ten-minute data and collapses on a chronological one. Same data, same network; the number that describes forecasting is the lower one.
"Deeper is not better" | Episode 7.1 | Measured on that dataset: 64-32 reached R² 0.380, while 512-256-128-64 reached 0.353. Capacity is not accuracy.
:::

Four more are demonstrated in Modules 7 and 8's labs and widgets, each with the seed that
reproduces it:

:::cards
"Do not leak across the split" | Episode 7.3 lab | the same k-NN scores R² 0.53 on a random split of autocorrelated data and −2.24 chronologically
"A surrogate is valid in its range" | Episode 8.4 lab | error inside the sampled box ≈ 1 kWh/m²; outside it, 5 to 20 times that, with no warning from the model
"A PINN enforces physics only where it checks" | Episode 8.5 lab | max error 0.0005 inside [0, 1]; the residual grows immediately past x = 1
"Beat persistence or you learned nothing" | Episode 8.3 lab | persistence R² 0.47; sensors and clock 0.13; one lag feature 0.54 — all on the same chronological split
:::
