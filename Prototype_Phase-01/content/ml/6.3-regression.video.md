:::scene{type=title}
narration: |
  Welcome to lesson six point three, regression: predicting a number. The question for
  this lesson is a real one. Can we predict a building's annual energy use from its
  physical, operational and environmental characteristics? Regression is the simplest
  well-understood way to try. You will see how a line is fitted, how one feature becomes several,
  and how to read the four numbers that tell you whether the model is any good.
:::

:::scene{type=compare}
heading: Regression or classification?
left:
  title: Regression — a number
  points:
    - Building features → 1,370,845 kWh
    - A continuous scale
    - Use it unless the decision really needs a category
right:
  title: Classification — a category
  points:
    - Building features → Low / Medium / High
    - One of a fixed set of labels
    - Use it when the decision is a class — next lesson
verdict: Do not force a number into arbitrary bands when the number would serve the decision better.
narration: |
  First, which kind of question you have. Regression predicts a number on a continuous
  scale: this building will use one million three hundred and seventy thousand kilowatt
  hours. Classification predicts a category: low, medium or high. If the decision needs
  a number, use regression. Do not chop a number into arbitrary bands just because
  categories feel simpler; you throw away information and you invent boundaries.
  Classification is the next lesson.
:::

:::scene{type=cards}
heading: Three symbols you will see everywhere
items:
  - title: X
    text: The input features, also called predictors. Building area, height, year built.
    tag: what you know
  - title: y
    text: The observed outcome, also called the target. Measured annual energy use.
    tag: the truth
  - title: ŷ
    text: The model's prediction, said "y hat". Estimated annual energy use.
    tag: the guess
narration: |
  Three symbols, and they will follow you through the rest of the course. Capital X is
  the input features, also called predictors: building area, height, year built. Little
  y is the observed outcome, also called the target: the measured annual energy use.
  And y with a hat on it is the model's prediction. The whole game is making y hat as
  close to y as possible, on buildings the model has not seen.
:::

:::scene{type=diagram id=regression-line}
heading: One line, chosen to make the gaps as small as possible
points:
  - ŷ = w·x + w₀ — a slope and an intercept
  - Residual = y − ŷ — the vertical gap for each point
  - Positive residual — the model guessed too low
  - Least squares — pick the line that makes the squared gaps smallest
narration: |
  Here is linear regression with one feature. Each dot is a building: its feature
  along the bottom, its energy use up the side. The line is the model: a slope, w, and
  an intercept, w zero. For each dot, the vertical gap between the dot and the line is
  the residual: observed minus predicted. Positive means the model guessed too low;
  negative, too high. Fitting means choosing the one line that makes the sum of the
  squared gaps as small as possible. That is all the word regression means here.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=build}
text: ŷ = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + wₙxₙ + w₀
sub: From one feature to many. One coefficient per predictor. Area, height and year built enter side by side, not one after another.
narration: |
  From one feature to many. Multiple regression uses several predictors together, with
  one coefficient per predictor. Area times its weight, plus height times its weight,
  plus year built times its weight, plus the intercept. The features enter side by
  side, all at once, not one after another. Each coefficient says how the prediction
  moves with that feature, while the others are held where they are. Which is exactly
  why a coefficient changes when you add or remove a feature.
:::

:::scene{type=cards}
heading: Where the predictors come from
items:
  - title: Geometric
    text: Area, height, building length. Physical characteristics of the building.
    tag: shape
  - title: Operational and program
    text: Year built, occupants, what the building is used for.
    tag: use
  - title: Environmental
    text: Tree canopy, land-surface temperature. The surroundings.
    tag: context
narration: |
  For a building, the predictors fall into three groups. Geometric: area, height,
  length, the physical shape. Operational and program: year built, occupants, what the
  building is for. Environmental: tree canopy, the surface temperature of the site, the
  surroundings. All three groups go into X together. And notice what does not go in:
  the observed energy use, y. That is the answer the model is trying to predict, so it
  is never available as an input.
:::

:::scene{type=cards}
heading: Four numbers that tell you how good the model is
items:
  - title: Residual
    text: y − ŷ for one building. The signed gap. Look at all of them, not just the average.
    tag: per building
  - title: MAE
    text: The average size of the residuals, in kWh. Signs cannot cancel. Lower is better.
    tag: typical miss
  - title: RMSE
    text: Root mean squared error. Same units, but big misses are punished harder.
    tag: big misses
  - title: R²
    text: The share of the variation the model explains, on the data it was evaluated on.
    tag: 0 to 1
narration: |
  Four numbers. The residual is the gap for one building; look at all of them, not just
  the average. M A E, the mean absolute error, is the typical size of a miss, in kilowatt
  hours, and lower is better. R M S E, the root mean squared error, is in the same units
  but punishes big misses much harder, so if the two disagree, you have a few buildings
  the model gets badly wrong. And R squared is the share of the variation the model
  explains, between zero and one, on the data it was evaluated on. That last clause is
  the whole next quiz.
:::

:::scene{type=quiz}
question: Your model's R² rises when you add a feature. What have you learned?
options:
  - The new feature causes energy use to change
  - The fit improved on this data — which says nothing yet about unseen buildings
  - The model is now reliable enough to report
answer: 1
pause: 5
explain: Adding a feature can only raise R² on the training data, even when the feature is noise. Reliability is measured on buildings the model never saw. And a fit, however good, never establishes cause.
narration: |
  A check. Your model's R squared rises when you add a feature. What have you learned?
  A: the new feature causes energy use to change. B: the fit improved on this data,
  which says nothing yet about unseen buildings. C: the model is now reliable enough to
  report. Pause and pick.
after: |
  It is B. Adding a feature can only push R squared up on the data it is fitted to, even
  if the feature is pure noise. Reliability is measured on buildings the model never saw.
  And a fit, however good, never establishes cause. A high R squared is an invitation to
  check further, not a conclusion.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=warn}
text: Predicts well / is not the same as / would change if you changed it.
sub: The model has found that taller buildings on this campus use more energy. It has not found that adding a floor would use more.
narration: |
  One more distinction, because it is the one designers most want to skip. A regression
  finds that taller buildings on this campus use more energy. It has not found that
  adding a floor to a given building would use more. The first is a pattern across
  buildings. The second is a claim about intervening on one, and this data cannot
  support it. Predictive relationships do not establish that changing a feature would
  change the outcome.
:::

:::scene{type=recap}
items:
  - Regression predicts a number; classification predicts a category
  - X the inputs, y the truth, ŷ the prediction
  - The line minimises the sum of squared residuals
  - Many features — one coefficient each, side by side
  - MAE for the typical miss, RMSE for big misses, R² for the share explained — on that data
narration: |
  To remember. Regression predicts a number; classification predicts a category. X the
  inputs, y the truth, y hat the prediction. The fitted line is the one that makes the
  sum of the squared residuals smallest. With many features there is one coefficient
  each, side by side. And M A E for the typical miss, R M S E for the big misses, R
  squared for the share explained, always on the data it was evaluated on.
:::
