:::scene{type=title}
narration: |
  Welcome to module six, machine learning, and to lesson six point one. So far in this
  course you have written the rules yourself: an if statement, a loop, a formula.
  Machine learning turns that around. You give the computer examples, and it finds the
  rule. This lesson names the four kinds of learning, tells you which one this course
  uses almost everywhere, and walks through the eight steps that take you from a raw
  table to a model somebody can rely on.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=bulb}
text: You do not write the rule. / You show examples, and the rule is found.
sub: Input and correct output, hundreds or thousands of times. The rule the computer finds is called a model.
narration: |
  Here is the whole idea in one sentence. In ordinary programming you write the rule:
  if the building is taller than five metres, expect a higher heating load. In machine
  learning you do not write the rule. You show the computer many examples, each an
  input with its correct output, and it finds a rule that maps one to the other. That
  rule is called a model. The rest of this course is about how good that rule is, and
  how you would know.
:::

:::scene{type=cards}
heading: Four kinds of learning
items:
  - title: Supervised
    text: The examples come with correct answers. Predict heating load from building features.
    tag: answers given
  - title: Unsupervised
    text: No answers. Find structure. Cluster buildings by their consumption pattern.
    tag: no answers
  - title: Semi-supervised
    text: A few labels, mostly unlabelled. 200 labelled facades, 20,000 photos.
    tag: a few answers
  - title: Reinforcement
    text: Learns from the consequences of actions. An HVAC control policy over a season.
    tag: trial and reward
narration: |
  There are four kinds of learning, and the difference is what the examples come with.
  Supervised: every example comes with its correct answer, like a building's features
  and its measured heating load. Unsupervised: no answers at all; the model looks for
  structure, like grouping buildings by how they use energy. Semi-supervised: a few
  labelled examples and a great many unlabelled ones, like two hundred facade photos
  with the material written down and twenty thousand without. And reinforcement
  learning: no examples of right answers, only consequences, like a heating control
  policy that is rewarded for comfort and penalised for energy over a season.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=check}
text: Almost everything in Modules 6 to 8 / is supervised.
sub: You have inputs. You have the matching correct outputs. You ask a model to learn the mapping between them.
narration: |
  Here is the one you need. Almost everything in modules six, seven and eight is
  supervised learning. You have inputs. You have the matching correct outputs. You ask

narration: |
  Before the workflow, a wider view. Where does machine learning actually enter
  architectural practice, and at which stage? The most complete answer at the time of
  writing is a twenty twenty five review by Lystbæk, which read two hundred and thirty
  papers on machine learning in building design and sorted them by domain, by method,
  and by design stage. Two findings matter here. First, the work clusters in two
  domains: building performance, which is mostly predicting energy, ninety nine papers,
  and automatic design generation, which is mostly floor plans, seventy one. Design
  evaluation, structural design and B I M automation make up the rest. Second, most of
  it targets the early design phase, because that is where a prediction can still
  change the design cheaply. The review draws this on the curve every architecture
  student knows: the ability to influence a design falls over a project while the cost
  of a change rises. A machine learning driven process moves the effort earlier still,
  to decide sooner, more accurately, and with less work. And it keeps people in the
  loop: in the review's words, human involvement is essential for training the
  machine, explaining outcomes, and sustaining A I systems.
:::

:::scene{type=steps}
heading: The complete workflow — eight steps
steps:
  - Frame the question — what decision, at what unit, and what would a useful answer look like
  - Collect and audit — coverage, missing values, duplicates, who is represented
  - Preprocess — clean, scale, encode; fit every transform on training data only
  - "*Split — by building, site or time, not by row; hold out a test set"
  - Train a baseline — the simplest model that could work
  - Tune — every choice on the validation set, never the test set
  - Evaluate once — on the untouched test set, with its range
  - Deploy and monitor — the world moves; a fitted model does not
narration: |
  Now the workflow, eight steps. One: frame the question. What decision does this
  inform, at what unit, and what would a useful answer look like? Two: collect and
  audit the data. Coverage, missing values, duplicates, and who is represented. Three:
  preprocess. Clean, scale, encode, and fit every transform on training data only.
  Four: split. By building, site or time, not merely by row, and hold out a test set
  you then leave alone. Five: train a baseline, the simplest model that could work.
  Six: tune, with every choice made on the validation set, never the test set. Seven:
  evaluate once, on the untouched test set, with the range stated. Eight: deploy and
  monitor, because the world moves and a fitted model does not.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=warn}
text: Steps 1 to 4 decide most of the outcome.
sub: The modelling step gets most of the attention. It is rarely where a project is won or lost.
narration: |
  And the lesson inside the list. Steps one to four, framing, auditing, preprocessing
  and splitting, decide most of the outcome. The modelling step, choosing and training
  the algorithm, gets most of the attention in articles and in class. It is rarely
  where a project is won or lost. A brilliant model on a badly framed question with a
  leaky split is worthless, and you will not know it until it fails.
:::
