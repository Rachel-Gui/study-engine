## Central question

What kinds of learning exist, and what are the steps between a raw dataset and a
model somebody actually relies on?

:::narration
Machine learning is not one thing. The first useful distinction is what the data
gives you. If every example comes with a correct answer attached, that is supervised
learning, and it covers almost everything in this course. If it does not, you are
looking for structure rather than predicting a target, and that is unsupervised
learning. Two more sit at the edges: semi-supervised, where a few examples are
labelled and most are not, and reinforcement learning, where a model learns from the
consequences of its own actions rather than from a fixed dataset.
:::

## Four kinds of learning

:::cards
Supervised | Examples come with correct answers | Predict energy use from building features
Unsupervised | No answers — find structure | Cluster buildings by consumption pattern
Semi-supervised | A few labels, mostly unlabelled | 200 labelled facades, 20,000 photos
Reinforcement | Learns from consequences of actions | HVAC control policy over a season
:::

:::figure{id=learning_types_map caption="Figure 1. Four learning setups organized by the signal available to the model, with one architectural example for each. Original course diagram."}
:::

:::keyidea
Almost everything in Modules 6 to 8 is **supervised**: you have inputs, you have the
matching correct outputs, and you are asking a model to learn the mapping between
them.
:::

The same building question can be framed several ways, and the framing decides which
kind of learning you need and what data you must have:

:::cards
"What will this design's heating load be?" | supervised — regression | inputs: geometry, glazing, climate; output: a number in kWh/m² — Lesson 6.3
"What material is this facade?" | supervised — classification | inputs: a photo; output: one of a fixed set of labels — Lessons 6.4 and 8.1
"Which buildings on campus behave alike?" | unsupervised — clustering | inputs: a year of meter readings each; no labels — the groups are the answer
"Label 20,000 photos from 200 labelled ones" | semi-supervised | the structure of the unlabelled photos does most of the work
"How should the HVAC respond, hour by hour?" | reinforcement | no examples of right answers; a reward for comfort, a penalty for energy
:::

Lystbæk's (2025) review of machine learning in building design sorts methods the same
way — supervised (from decision trees and gradient boosting to CNNs and GNNs),
unsupervised (k-means), reinforcement and semi-supervised — and adds a fifth group,
**generative ML** (GANs, VAEs, diffusion models, transformers), which is Module 2 of
this course. Keep the map in mind: the generative tools you met first are one branch
of the same field.

## Where machine learning enters building design

:::narration
Before the workflow, a wider view: where does machine learning actually enter
architectural practice, and at which stage? The most complete answer at the time of
writing is a 2025 review by Lystbæk, which read two hundred and thirty papers on
machine learning in building design and sorted them by domain, purpose, method and
design stage. Two findings matter for this course. First, the work clusters in two
domains: building performance, which is mostly predicting energy, and automatic
design generation, which is mostly floor plans. Second, most of it targets the early
design phase, because that is where a prediction can still change the design cheaply.
The review draws this as a version of the curve every architecture student knows: the
ability to influence a design falls over a project while the cost of a change rises,
and a machine learning driven process moves the effort earlier still, with the aim of
deciding sooner, more accurately, and with less work.
:::

Lystbæk (2025) reviewed 230 papers, selected from 2,706 records, on ML in the
architectural building design process, and tagged each by **domain and purpose**, by
**method**, and by **design stage** — pre-design, schematic design, design development,
construction documentation, procurement, construction administration and operations.
Five domains hold the field: building performance (99 papers, of which energy
prediction is 64), automatic design generation (71, of which floor-plan generation is
30), design evaluation (26), structural design (20) and BIM automation (14). And the
studies "were mainly applied in the early design phase, where influencing design
choices is still beneficial and feasible."

:::figure{id=ml_design_stages caption="Figure 2. Design stages, the falling ability to influence a design and the rising cost of a change, the ML-driven effort curve, and where the five domains mostly sit. Original diagram after Lystbæk (2025), CC BY 4.0; the placement of the domains along the stages is the course's reading of the review."}
:::

The review's own picture of an ML pipeline — collect data (tabular, text or images),
build and preprocess a dataset, train, evaluate and refine until the result is
satisfactory, deploy — is the eight-step workflow below with the framing and the
split made explicit. Its ML-driven process curve extends the familiar MacLeamy
diagram: the point of moving effort earlier is "to enable earlier, more accurate
decisions and to reduce workload through automation." It also keeps people in the
loop: "fully autonomous operation without human support remains a topic of
discussion", and "human involvement is essential for training the machine, explaining
outcomes, and sustaining AI systems."

:::keyidea
**Prediction is worth most where a change is still cheap.** That is why this module's
examples are early-design questions — a heating load from geometry, a load class from
a few features — and why the answer has to come with its range.
:::

:::predict
A practice wants to add one ML tool. Where, by the review's evidence, will a good
prediction change the most?
- Construction documentation — the data is richest there | Richer data, but the design is nearly fixed; a prediction there confirms rather than changes.
- *Schematic design — the ability to influence is high and a change is still cheap | Yes. Most reviewed applications target the early phase for exactly this reason.
- Operations — the building is real and can be measured | Measurement is best there, but the design decisions are long made; that is monitoring, not design support.
:::

## The complete workflow

:::figure{id=ml_workflow_map caption="Figure 3. The workflow grouped into framing, data, modelling and evidence. A weak data split or leakage invalidates every later result. Original course diagram after the workflow categories synthesized in Lystbæk (2025)."}
:::

:::workflow
Frame the question | What decision does this inform, at what unit, and what would a useful answer look like? | 
Collect and audit | Check coverage, missing values, duplicates, labelling rules, and who is represented. | 
Preprocess | Clean, scale, encode, and engineer features. Fit every transform on training data only. | 
Split | Separate by building, site, or time — not merely by row. Hold out a test set and leave it alone. | 
Train a baseline | The simplest model that could work. If it wins, you are finished. | 
Tune | Use the validation set for every choice. Never the test set. | 
Evaluate once | Report on the untouched test set, with the range and the failure conditions stated. | 
Deploy and monitor | Watch for drift. The world moves; a fitted model does not. | 
:::

:::keyidea
Steps 1 to 4 decide most of the outcome. The modelling step, which gets most of the
attention, is rarely where a project is won or lost.
:::

The review names the problems that survive a good workflow, and they are the ones the
rest of this course keeps returning to: data scarcity and the cost of collecting
more, bias inherited from the data, generative tools that "fabricated results", the
skill a prompt requires, copyright and privacy, and the lack of domain-specific
metrics — a generated plan that scores well on pixels can still break the rules a
planner would check first.
