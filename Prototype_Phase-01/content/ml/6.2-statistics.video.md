:::scene{type=title}

  Welcome to lesson six point two, why statistics and probability matter. This is a
  short lesson with no code in it, and it may be the most important one in the module.
  Three ideas: variation, sampling, and uncertainty. Every model you build from now on
  is judged with these three, and every mistake people make with models comes from
  forgetting one of them.

:::scene{type=idea icon=question}

narration: |
  Start with the question the lesson answers. Why does a model that fits the data well
  still tell you very little on its own? Because a fit is a statement about this data:
  these rows, this sample, this campus. The three ideas in this lesson are what you
  need to turn it into a careful statement about the world.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=warn}
text: Moving together / is not one causing the other.
sub: One drives the other. Or a third drives both. Or the sample was drawn that way. A model that predicts well is exploiting association.
narration: |
  Now the sentence everyone has heard and few people apply: correlation is not
  causation. Two quantities can move together for three reasons. One drives the other.
  A third thing drives both. Or the sample was drawn in a way that makes them move
  together. A model that predicts well is exploiting association. It has not
  established mechanism. Roof area and heating load, from module five, is the perfect
  example: a strong correlation, and height driving both.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=eye}
text: A coefficient is a property of the fit, / not of the building.
sub: It says how the prediction changes with one feature while the other included features are held constant. Change the feature set, and every coefficient changes.
narration: |
  This matters for the models coming next. A coefficient in a regression tells you how
  the model's prediction changes with one feature, while the other features in the
  model are held constant. Change which features are in the model, and every
  coefficient changes. So a coefficient is a property of the fit, not a property of
  the building. In six point six you will watch this happen with your own eyes.
:::

:::scene{type=compare}
heading: What a p-value is, and what it is not
left:
  title: What it is
  points:
    - The probability of data this extreme, if nothing were going on
    - A statement about the data, under an assumption
right:
  title: What it is not
  points:
    - The probability that your hypothesis is true
    - A measure of how much the effect matters
    - A statement about cause
verdict: Report the effect size and its range, not just whether a threshold was crossed.
narration: |
  A p value. What it is: the probability of seeing data this extreme if nothing were
  going on. A statement about the data, under an assumption. What it is not: the
  probability that your hypothesis is true. A measure of how much the effect matters.
  A statement about cause. A statistically significant effect can be far too small to
  matter for a design decision. So report the effect size and its range, not just
  whether a threshold was crossed.
:::

:::scene{type=quiz}
question: A feature's effect on annual energy use has p = 0.001. The effect itself is 0.2 kWh/m² per year. What should you report?
options:
  - Significant, so the feature matters — report the p-value
  - The effect size, 0.2, and its range — the p-value only says it is unlikely to be chance
  - Nothing — a p-value that small cannot be trusted
answer: 1
pause: 5
explain: With enough rows almost anything is significant. Significant means unlikely by chance, not important. An effect of 0.2 kWh/m² is real and irrelevant to any design decision. Lead with the size and the range.

:::scene{type=list}
heading: Three habits, from the three ideas
items:
  - Never report a mean alone — give the range, or show the distribution
  - Say what the data is a sample of, and what it excludes, in the first sentence
  - Put a range on every prediction, and say which error the decision can least afford
  - Say "associated with", not "causes", unless you ran an experiment
narration: |
  The three ideas become three habits, plus one. Never report a mean alone: give the
  range, or show the distribution. Say what the data is a sample of, and what it
  excludes, in the first sentence of any result. Put a range on every prediction, and
  say which kind of error the decision can least afford. And say associated with, not
  causes, unless you actually ran an experiment.
:::
