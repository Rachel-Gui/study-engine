  the ones after it.

:::scene{type=diagram id=uw-baseline-coefficients}
heading: The fitted baseline — three predictors
points:
  - Area_m — +109 kWh per area unit
  - Height — +27,206 kWh per height unit
  - Year_Built — +6,977 kWh per calendar year
  - Intercept — an offset, not a building
narration: |
  Here is what the fit produced, rounded for reading. Three coefficients. Area: plus one
  hundred and nine kilowatt hours per unit of area. Height: plus twenty seven thousand
  per unit of height. Year built: plus about seven thousand per calendar year, so newer
  buildings are predicted to use more, on this campus, in this fit. And an intercept of
  minus thirteen million, which is an offset that makes the line work, not a building
  with zero area built in year zero.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=eye}
text: A coefficient is an adjusted association.
sub: The predicted change for a one-unit increase, while the other included predictors are held constant. Add a predictor, and every coefficient can change. Size is not importance.
narration: |
  Read a coefficient the careful way. It is the predicted change in energy use for a one
  unit increase in that feature, while the other predictors in the model are held
  constant. That makes it an adjusted association, not a causal effect. Two warnings.
  Add a predictor and every coefficient can change, because held constant now means
  something different. And size is not importance: one unit of area, one unit of height
  and one calendar year are not comparable things.
:::

  A check. What does a negative test R squared tell you about this baseline? A: the

  It is B. R squared compares the model's squared errors with those of the simplest
  possible guess, the mean. Zero means no better than the mean; negative means worse.

:::scene{type=idea icon=bulb}
text: A disappointing result on unseen data / beats a flattering one on training data.
sub: Three predictors, 91 buildings, unresolved units and a campus full of very different programs. The model is telling you what the data can and cannot support.
narration: |
  Why is this a good outcome? Because it is true. Three predictors, ninety one training
  buildings, some units nobody can confirm, and a campus with a hospital, a library, a
  stadium and a warehouse in the same table. The model is telling you what this data can
  and cannot support. A pipeline that reported a high score here would be the one to
  worry about, because you would have to ask how it got it.
:::

:::scene{type=list}
heading: Try other feature sets — but predict first
items:
  - Pick a new combination — the same fixed split keeps comparisons fair
  - Write down what you expect the test error to do, before you run it
  - Read the new coefficients — which ones changed, and why?
  - Then compare the test error with your prediction
narration: |
  The lesson page has a feature lab where you try other combinations on the same fixed
  test buildings. The rule for using it: predict first. Pick a combination, write down
  what you expect the test error to do, then run it. Read the new coefficients and notice
  which ones changed, now that different things are held constant. Then compare the test
  error with what you wrote down. More features does not reliably mean a better model,
  and this is where you get to feel that.
:::
