:::scene{type=title}
narration: |
  Welcome to lesson eight point four: physics informed learning, surrogate models, and
  implicit neural representations. Every model so far learned from examples and nothing
  else. But in building science you often know something the data does not say: heat
  flows from hot to cold, energy is conserved, more glass never reduces solar gain. This
  lesson is about three ways to put what you know into a model, and about the one
  question you must ask of all of them: where is it valid?
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=question}
text: Fast feedback, / physically plausible, / where the model was never trained?
sub: A simulation is exact and slow. A surrogate is fast and valid in a range. A PINN puts the equation in the loss. An INR stores a field as a function. All four need validating.
narration: |
  The question: can a model give fast simulation feedback while staying physically
  plausible under conditions it was never trained on? Four tools, and the lesson walks the
  spectrum. A simulation is exact and slow. A surrogate is fast, and valid inside a
  range. A physics informed network puts the equation itself into the loss. And an
  implicit neural representation stores a whole field as a function. Every one of them
  needs validating, and they fail in different places.
:::

:::scene{type=cards}
heading: Four ways physics enters a model — the review's map
items:
  - title: Physics-informed inputs
    text: Simulation results become features — a degree-day, an EnergyPlus run. Cheapest; valuable when measurements are scarce.
    tag: inputs
  - title: Physics-informed loss
    text: Data loss plus λ times a physics residual. The PINN route. A soft penalty — the laws are only approximately satisfied.
    tag: loss
  - title: Physics-informed architecture
    text: The network's structure encodes the physics — grey-box layers, neural ODEs, a bounded output. Strict; extrapolates better.
    tag: architecture
  - title: Physics-informed ensembles
    text: A physics model plus a data model that learns its residual. Interpretable; hard to say what each part contributed.
    tag: ensembles
narration: |
  Physics informed machine learning is an umbrella term, not one method, and the most
  useful map of it for buildings is a twenty twenty five review by Ma, Jiang, Hu and
  Chen. It starts from three weaknesses of a purely data driven energy model: it needs
  a lot of high quality data, its predictions can be physically inconsistent, and it
  cannot be trusted outside the range it was trained on. Then it sorts every remedy
  into four routes, by where the physics enters. As inputs: simulation results become
  features, a degree day, an EnergyPlus run. The cheapest route, and valuable when
  measurements are scarce. As a loss: the data loss plus lambda times a physics
  residual. That is the P I N N route, and it is a soft penalty: the laws are only
  approximately satisfied. As architecture: the structure of the network encodes the
  physics, a grey box layer, a neural O D E, an output that cannot go outside its
  bounds. Strict, and it extrapolates better, but the rules encoded so far are simple.
  And as ensembles: a physics model plus a data model that learns what the physics
  missed. Interpretable, though hard to say what each part contributed.
:::

:::scene{type=diagram id=piml_taxonomy}
heading: What each route buys, and what it costs
points:
  - "More glass never reduces solar gain — a bound is the architecture route, by construction"
  - A PINN is one instance of the loss route — not a synonym for physics-informed ML
  - λ sets how hard the physics pulls; too weak and it is ignored, too strong and the data is
  - A pure surrogate is outside the map — nearest the inputs route, valid only in its sampled range
narration: |
  Here is the map with the trade offs. Two things follow from it. A bound, more glass
  never reduces solar gain, is a small case of the architecture route: enforced by
  construction, not by a penalty. And a P I N N is one instance of the loss route, not
  a synonym for physics informed learning. The review treats the physics informed
  neural networks of Raissi and colleagues as the best known way to write that loss,
  with a weight, lambda, that sets how hard the physics term pulls. Too weak and the
  physics is ignored; too strong and the data is. One more thing is missing from the
  map on purpose. A pure surrogate, trained on simulator runs to stand in for the
  simulator, is outside the review's scope. It sits nearest the inputs route, and it
  is valid only inside its sampled range, which is the second half of this lesson.
:::

:::scene{type=diagram id=pinn_loss}
heading: A PINN is a normal network with an unusual loss
points:
  - Coordinates in — x, t — and a value out, u(x, t)
  - Data loss — the gap at sensor points, if there are any
  - PDE residual — how badly the output violates the equation, at collocation points
  - Boundary loss — the known values at the edges
narration: |
  A physics informed neural network is a normal network with an unusual loss. In go
  coordinates, out comes a value, say a temperature at a place and time. The loss has
  three parts. A data loss: the gap at sensor points, if you have any. An equation
  residual: take the network's derivatives, computed exactly by automatic
  differentiation, plug them into the governing equation, and measure how badly it is
  violated at a cloud of check points called collocation points. And a boundary loss: the
  known values at the edges. Weights on the three terms, and you train as usual.
:::

:::scene{type=code}
heading: The entire mechanism, one parameter instead of thousands
code: |
  # Equation: u''(x) = -1 on (0, 1), with u(0) = u(1) = 0
  # Trial solution u = a * x * (1 - x), so u'' = -2a, and the ends are 0 for any a
  a, lr = 0.0, 0.1

  def residual(a):
      return -2 * a - (-1)          # u'' minus the right-hand side

  for step in range(1, 7):
      r = residual(a)
      loss = r ** 2                 # physics loss: no data anywhere
      a = a - lr * (2 * r * -2)     # gradient step, chain rule
output: |
  1 residual 1.0 -> a 0.4
  2 residual 0.2 -> a 0.48
  3 residual 0.04 -> a 0.496
  ...
  exact a = 0.5, so u(x) = 0.5 x (1 - x)
note: A uniformly loaded beam, or a wall with a heat source. No dataset — the equation is the teacher. A real PINN swaps a for a network's weights and one point for a cloud of them. The residual remains part of the training loss.
narration: |
  Here is the whole mechanism at its smallest. The equation says the curvature of u is
  minus one, and u is zero at both ends: a uniformly loaded beam, or a wall with a heat
  source. The trial solution has one unknown, a. Its curvature is minus two a. The
  residual is minus two a, minus the minus one the equation asks for. Square it: that is
  the loss, and there is no data anywhere. Step downhill: a goes to zero point four, zero
  point four eight, zero point four nine six, closing on the exact answer, one half. A
  real P I N N swaps a for a network's weights and one point for a cloud of collocation
  points. The residual remains part of the training loss.
:::

:::scene{type=quiz}
question: You train a PINN with the boundary term switched off. The equation residual reaches nearly zero. Is the solution right?
options:
  - Yes — zero residual means the equation is satisfied
  - No — without boundary conditions the equation does not pin down a unique solution
  - Only if the network is large enough
answer: 1
pause: 5
explain: Satisfied, yes — but the equation alone has infinitely many solutions. Add any straight line a + bx to the answer and the curvature is unchanged. The boundary term is what selects one. Size is irrelevant; the problem is under-determined, not under-fitted.
narration: |
  A check. You train a P I N N with the boundary term switched off. The equation residual
  reaches nearly zero. Is the solution right? A: yes, zero residual means the equation is
  satisfied. B: no, without boundary conditions the equation does not pin down a unique
  solution. C: only if the network is large enough. Pause and pick.
after: |
  It is B. The equation is satisfied, and the equation alone has infinitely many
  solutions: add any straight line to the answer and the curvature does not change. The
  boundary term is what selects the one you want. Which one you got without it is an
  accident of initialisation. Size is irrelevant; the problem is under determined, not
  under fitted.
:::

:::scene{type=diagram id=surrogate_pipeline}
heading: Surrogate models — the simulator is in the training set
points:
  - Simulator — CFD or EnergyPlus, hours per run
  - Dataset — N designs in, N results out; the range you sampled
  - Surrogate — an MLP, a GP or a CNN; milliseconds per query
  - Design loop — thousands of queries; and the check that closes the loop
narration: |
  The second tool, and the one you are most likely to build. A surrogate learns from a
  simulator instead of from measurements. Run the slow simulator, C F D or EnergyPlus,
  on a few hundred designs, hours per run. That gives a dataset: designs in, results
  out. Fit a network, or another fast model, to it. Now a query takes milliseconds
  instead of hours, and a design loop can ask thousands of questions. The last box is
  the important one: every so often, send a design back to the real simulator and check.
:::

:::scene{type=diagram id=interpolation_region}
heading: Inside the cloud it interpolates. Outside, it guesses.
points:
  - Training runs — the cloud of designs the simulator actually ran
  - Query A, inside — interpolating between runs it has seen; error is measurable
  - Query B, outside — extrapolating with a smooth function; error is unknown
  - Marking where the range ends is part of the deliverable
narration: |
  Here is where surrogates fail, and it is not where people look. Each dot is a design the
  simulator actually ran. Query A sits inside the cloud: the surrogate is interpolating
  between runs it has seen, and its error is measurable. Query B sits outside: the
  surrogate is guessing with a smooth function, and it will give you a confident number
  with no basis at all. A fast surrogate replaces the simulator inside the range it was
  fitted on, and nowhere else. Marking where that range ends is part of the deliverable.
:::

:::scene{type=diagram id=inr}
heading: Implicit neural representations — a field stored as a function
points:
  - A grid stores values at fixed cells; resolution fixed at creation
  - An INR stores a network — coordinates in, value out
  - Query anywhere, at any resolution; the mesh is gone
  - Not physics-informed unless you add the physics to its loss
narration: |
  The third tool. A grid or a mesh stores a field, a temperature or a pressure or a wind
  speed, as values at fixed cells, and the resolution is fixed the day you make it. An
  implicit neural representation stores the field as a network instead: coordinates in,
  value out, query anywhere at any resolution, and the mesh is gone. It is a way of
  storing a field, and only that. It is not physics informed unless you add the physics
  to its loss.
:::

:::scene{type=quiz}
question: You train a network on coordinates to reproduce a wind field around a building. Is it physics-informed?
options:
  - Yes — it represents a physical field
  - Not unless you added physical constraints, such as equation residuals, to the loss
  - Yes, because it replaces a CFD solver
answer: 1
pause: 5
explain: Representing a physical quantity is not the same as being constrained by physics. Without a physics term in the loss, the network is a very flexible curve fit through the CFD results — an INR, or a surrogate. Say which one you built.
narration: |
  A check. You train a network on coordinates to reproduce a wind field around a
  building. Is it physics informed? A: yes, it represents a physical field. B: not unless
  you added physical constraints, such as equation residuals, to the loss. C: yes, because
  it replaces a C F D solver. Pause and pick.
after: |
  It is B. Representing a physical quantity is not the same as being constrained by
  physics. Without a physics term in the loss, the network is a very flexible curve fit
  through the C F D results: an implicit representation, or a surrogate. Both are useful.
  Neither is physics informed. Say which one you built, and reviewers will trust the rest.
:::

:::scene{type=list}
heading: Validation — what every one of these owes you
items:
  - Compare against independent simulation or measurement, not the training runs
  - Test other geometries, scales, climates and boundary conditions
  - Report field error, extreme-value error, physical residual and uncertainty
  - Mark interpolation and extrapolation regions on every result
narration: |
  Whichever tool you build, it owes you the same validation. Compare against independent
  simulation or measurement, never against the runs it trained on. Test other geometries,
  scales, climates and boundary conditions. Report not just the average field error but
  the extreme value error, the physical residual, and the uncertainty. And mark the
  interpolation and extrapolation regions on every result, so nobody uses the model where
  it is guessing.
:::
