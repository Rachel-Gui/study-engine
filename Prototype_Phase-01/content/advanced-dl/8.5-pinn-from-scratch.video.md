  Welcome to lesson eight point five, hands on: a physics informed network from scratch.

:::scene{type=diagram id=pinn_problem}
heading: The problem — u″ = f on (0, 1), with u = 0 at both ends
points:
  - The equation — u″(x) = −π² sin(πx); curvature equals the source
  - Boundary conditions — u(0) = u(1) = 0; both faces held at ambient
  - Collocation points — 30 values of x where the residual is checked
  - The answer, u = sin(πx) — which the network is never shown
narration: |
  The problem. A wall one metre thick, both faces held at ambient, with a distributed heat
  source inside it. In equations: the curvature of the temperature profile equals the
  source, and the profile is zero at both ends. The exact answer is a sine curve. The
  network is never shown it. It is shown the source, the two boundary values, and thirty
  places along the wall where the equation will be checked. From that alone it has to
  find the curve.
:::

:::scene{type=steps}
heading: Seven steps
steps:
  - The problem, and the answer we pretend not to know
  - A tiny network, and its second derivative by hand
  - The physics-informed loss and its gradient
  - Train with Adam
  - Look at it
  - "*Break it — three ablations"
  - Data only, physics only, both
narration: |
  Seven steps. Write down the problem, and the exact answer that will only be used to
  grade the network. Build the tiny network and derive its second derivative by hand,
  using the chain rule from module seven. Assemble the loss, residual plus boundary, and
  its gradient. Train with Adam. Look at the result against the exact curve. Then break
  it: three experiments that remove one ingredient each. And finally the one that
  matters for real buildings: data only, physics only, and both together.
:::

:::scene{type=code}
heading: The loss — residual at the checkpoints, plus the two ends
code: |
  def loss(p):
      out  = forward(p, x_col)                 # value u and 2nd derivative u_xx
      u, u_xx = out[2], out[3]
      residual = u_xx - f(x_col)                  # how badly u'' = f fails
      L_pde = np.mean(residual ** 2)              # at the 30 checkpoints

      u0, u1 = forward(p, np.array([0.0, 1.0]))[2]
      L_bc  = u0 ** 2 + u1 ** 2                   # the two boundary values

      return L_pde + lam_bc * L_bc                # lam_bc = 10
note: Two numbers, added. The residual says "obey the equation here". The boundary term says "and be zero at the ends". Nothing says anything about the answer in between.
narration: |
  The heart of it is the loss, and it is two numbers added together. Run the thirty
  checkpoints through the network and take the second derivative. Subtract the source:
  that is the residual, how badly the equation fails at each point. Square it and average
  it. Then run the two end points through and square their values, because they should be
  zero. Add the two, with a weight of ten on the boundary term. Nothing in this function
  says anything about the answer. It says obey the equation here, and be zero at the ends.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=check}
text: Three decimals of the exact answer. / With no examples of the answer.
sub: The equation, two boundary values and thirty checkpoints were enough. Physics is enforced where you check it — and only there.
narration: |
  Train it, and it works. The network reproduces the exact solution to three decimal
  places, having never seen a single example of it. The equation, two boundary values and
  thirty checkpoints were enough. Now the other half of the sentence. Ask the trained
  network about x beyond one, outside the wall, and it stops obeying the equation the
  moment it leaves the collocation set. Physics is enforced where you check it, and only
  there.
:::

  - Adam cannot minimise two losses at once

narration: |
  Step seven, the experiment that decides whether a P I N N is worth the trouble on a real
  building. Four noisy measurements on their own: a curve that passes near the points and
  is wrong between them, maximum error about zero point two. The equation alone: exact,
  because in this toy problem the equation is exactly right, a luxury no building offers.
  The four points plus the equation: right everywhere, about zero point zero five. Read
  it carefully. When the physics is exact, data can only add noise. When the physics is
  approximate, which for a building it always is, data is what corrects it.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=warn}
text: Removing one term at a time / is the way to report a PINN.
sub: '"It works" says nothing. "Without the boundary term the error is 3.3, with it 0.0005" is a result. Each ablation says what that term bought.'
narration: |
  Which gives you the reporting rule. Removing one term at a time is the way to
  report a physics informed model. Each ablation says what that term bought. It works,
  says nothing. Without the boundary term the error is three point three, with it, zero
  point zero zero zero five: that is a result. And only three collocation points instead
  of thirty: another number, another sentence a reviewer can check.
:::

:::scene{type=quiz}
question: A colleague proposes a PINN for indoor-temperature prediction and says it "will respect the physics by construction". Which reply is accurate?
options:
  - Correct — a PINN cannot violate its equation
  - It will be penalised for violating the equation at the points you check, and it needs validation like any other model
  - PINNs only work for toy problems
answer: 1
pause: 5
explain: The physics is a loss term, not a constraint. The network minimises the residual; it does not zero it, and it enforces nothing where nothing was checked. PINNs have solved real problems — they simply need the same honesty about range and validation.
