:::scene{type=title}
narration: |
  Welcome to module eight, advanced deep learning for architecture, and to lesson eight
  point one: convolutional networks and computer vision. Everything in module seven took a
  row of numbers as input. A photograph is not a row of numbers; it is a grid, and where
  a pixel sits matters. This lesson is about the one trick that lets a network read a
  grid, what it makes possible for facades, plans and aerial images, and the two ways it
  goes wrong.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=question}
text: How does a network get from raw pixels / to a decision about a building?
sub: Pixels → local patterns → less resolution, more meaning → parts and objects → the answer you asked for.
narration: |
  The question: how does a network move from raw pixels to architectural features and
  decisions? In stages. Pixels first. Then local patterns: edges, corners, textures. Then
  fewer pixels but more meaning. Then parts and objects: a window, a door, a roofline.
  And at the end, a head that turns all of that into the answer you asked for: brick or
  glass, defect or fine, a box around every window. The whole stack is built from one
  operation, and that operation is the next scene.
:::

:::scene{type=diagram id=convolution}
heading: Convolution — one small filter, slid over every position
points:
  - A 3×3 kernel — nine weights
  - Multiply the window by the kernel, sum — one number
  - Slide it everywhere — a 4×4 feature map from a 6×6 image
  - Pool — keep the strongest response in each 2×2 block
narration: |
  Here is a convolution. Take a tiny grid of nine weights, called a kernel. Lay it over
  the top left corner of the image, multiply each pixel by the weight on top of it, add
  the nine products up, and you get one number. Slide the kernel one pixel to the right
  and do it again. Slide it over every position, and the numbers you collect form a
  feature map: a picture of where the pattern was found. Then pool: keep only the
  strongest response in each two by two block, so the map gets smaller and the detail
  that survives is the detail that mattered.
:::

:::scene{type=quiz}
question: A kernel has −1 in its top row, 0 in the middle row, and +1 in the bottom row. On a facade, where will it respond most strongly?
options:
  - Along the left and right sides of each window
  - Along the top and bottom edges of each window
  - Uniformly across the wall
answer: 1
pause: 5
explain: The kernel compares the row above with the row below, so it fires where brightness changes vertically — the top and bottom edges of a window. Left and right sides are vertical edges, which a kernel with −1 and +1 columns would find. A flat wall has no change, so the sum is zero.
narration: |
  A check. A kernel has minus one across its top row, zero in the middle, and plus one
  across the bottom. On a facade, where will it respond most strongly? A: along the left
  and right sides of each window. B: along the top and bottom edges of each window. C:
  uniformly across the wall. Pause and pick.
after: |
  It is B. The kernel compares the row above with the row below, so it fires where
  brightness changes vertically, at the top and bottom edges of a window. The left and
  right sides are vertical edges, and a kernel with minus one and plus one in its columns
  would find those. A flat wall has no change in any direction, so the sum is zero. Each
  kernel is a question, asked at every position.
:::

:::scene{type=number}
heading: Weight sharing — why this is not a detail
items:
  - value: "9"
    label: weights in one 3×3 kernel, used at every position
  - value: "36"
    label: weights a dense layer needs for one output on a 6×6 image — one per pixel
  - value: "billions"
    label: what a dense layer would need on a 224×224 photo. A convolution still needs nine.
    count: false
narration: |
  Why this is the trick and not a detail. A dense layer, the kind from module seven,
  needs one weight per input per output: on a six by six image, thirty six weights for a
  single output, and on a real two hundred and twenty four pixel photograph, billions. A
  convolution needs nine, used at every position. The same nine weights find a window in
  the corner and a window in the centre, because a pattern learned once is found
  anywhere. That is called weight sharing, and it is the reason vision networks are
  possible at all.
:::

:::scene{type=diagram id=cnn_stack}
heading: The stack — spatial size shrinks, meaning grows
points:
  - Early layers see pixels — edges and textures
  - Middle layers see parts — a window, a cornice
  - Late layers see the whole facade
  - The head — flatten, dense, the answer you asked for
narration: |
  Stack the operation. Convolution and relu, then convolution and pooling, again and
  again. With each stage the spatial size shrinks and the number of channels, the number
  of different patterns being detected, grows. Early layers see pixels: edges and
  textures. Middle layers see parts: a window, a cornice. Late layers see the whole
  facade. Then the head: flatten what is left, run it through a dense layer, and produce
  the answer. Change the head and the same stack classifies, detects or segments.
:::

:::scene{type=cards}
heading: Three kinds of answer
items:
  - title: Classification
    text: One label per image. Facade material — brick, render, glass.
    tag: what is it
  - title: Detection
    text: Boxes around things. Windows, doors, defects, signs.
    tag: where is it
  - title: Segmentation
    text: One label per pixel. Roof, wall, glazing, vegetation from aerial imagery.
    tag: which pixels
narration: |
  Three kinds of answer. Classification: one label for the whole image, like the facade
  material. Detection: boxes around things, windows, doors, defects, signs. Segmentation:
  a label for every pixel, roof, wall, glazing, vegetation, from an aerial image. The
  applications in architecture follow from these: facade surveys from street view,
  construction progress and safety monitoring, urban analysis from satellite imagery,
  accessibility evaluation. Same stack, different head, different labels.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=warn}
text: '"Brick" means the surface looks like brick.'
sub: The model has recognised an appearance. It has not established the wall assembly, its U-value, or its condition. Treat the output as a triage signal that routes a building to a human.
narration: |
  Now the two ways this goes wrong, and the first is about what the answer means. A
  facade classifier that reports brick has recognised a surface appearance. It has not
  established the wall assembly behind it, its U value, or its condition. It may also have
  learned the background, the sky, the season or the camera, instead of the wall. Treat
  the output as a triage signal that routes a building to a human, never as a survey
  result.
:::

:::scene{type=quiz}
question: A model classifies facade material from street-view images with 94% accuracy on a random image-level split. The images come from 600 buildings, 8 photos each. What is the right next step?
options:
  - Deploy it — 94% is high
  - Re-split by building so no building appears in both sets, and re-measure
  - Add more layers to push it past 95%
answer: 1
pause: 5
explain: With 8 photos per building, a random split almost certainly put the same building on both sides. The model recognised facades it had seen. Split by building, expect a lower number, and treat that lower number as the result.
narration: |
  The second way it goes wrong, as a check. A model classifies facade material from
  street view images with ninety four percent accuracy on a random, image level split.
  The images come from six hundred buildings, eight photos each. What is the right next
  step? A: deploy it, ninety four percent is high. B: re split by building so no building
  appears in both sets, and re measure. C: add more layers to push it past ninety five.
  Pause and pick.
after: |
  It is B. With eight photos per building, a random split almost certainly put the same
  building on both sides. The model recognised facades it had already seen. Split by
  building, expect a lower number, and treat that lower number as the result. You have
  now met this trap with rows, with sensor readings and with photographs. It is the same
  trap every time.
:::

:::scene{type=list}
heading: The risks, in one list
items:
  - Geographic and visual bias — the cities and styles in the training set
  - Cameras, lighting, occlusion, seasons and image quality all shift the pixels
  - The model may learn background context instead of the building
  - Visible material does not reveal the assembly behind it
  - Same facade in training and test — recognition, not generalization
narration: |
  The risks, in one list. Geographic and visual bias: the model knows the cities and
  styles it was trained on. Cameras, lighting, occlusion, seasons and image quality all
  move the pixels, and the model may not follow. It may learn the background instead of
  the building. Visible material does not reveal the assembly behind it. And the same
  facade in training and test measures recognition, not generalization. Every one of
  these is a question to ask of any vision paper before you believe its number.
:::

:::scene{type=recap}
items:
  - A convolution slides nine weights over every position and records where the pattern is
  - Pooling keeps the strongest response — less resolution, more meaning
  - Weight sharing — a pattern learned once is found anywhere; billions of weights become nine
  - Same stack, different head — classify, detect, segment
  - '"Brick" is an appearance, not an assembly — and split by building, never by image'
narration: |
  To remember. A convolution slides nine weights over every position and records where
  the pattern is. Pooling keeps the strongest response: less resolution, more meaning.
  Weight sharing means a pattern learned once is found anywhere, and billions of weights
  become nine. Same stack, different head: classify, detect, segment. And brick is an
  appearance, not an assembly, and you split by building, never by image.
:::
