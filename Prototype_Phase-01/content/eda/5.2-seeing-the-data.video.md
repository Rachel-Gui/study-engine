:::scene{type=diagram id=figure_anatomy}
heading: The parts of every plot
narration: |
  Matplotlib draws one element at a time and gives you control over all of it. The
  pattern is always the same. Make a figure and an axes. Call methods on the axes to
  draw. Then label it: a title, an x label, a y label, a legend. Watch the parts build.
  Once you know these names, every matplotlib example on the internet becomes
  readable.
:::

:::scene{type=code}
heading: A histogram, and a scatter with a third variable
code: |
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.5))

  ax[0].hist(df["heating_load"], bins=30)
  ax[0].set_xlabel("heating load (kWh/m²)")

  ax[1].scatter(df["relative_compactness"], df["heating_load"],
                c=df["overall_height"])          # colour by a third column
  ax[1].set_xlabel("compactness"); ax[1].set_ylabel("heating load")
note: The bin count is a choice — try three. Colour by a third column before you believe the shape of a scatter.
narration: |
  Two plots in eight lines. On the left, a histogram of the heating load: the two
  clusters appear immediately. The number of bins is a choice, so try three different
  counts before you trust a shape. On the right, a scatter of compactness against
  heating load, coloured by a third column, the building height. That colouring is the
  most important line in the lesson, and here is why.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=eye}
text: Colouring by a third variable / changed the story.
sub: Compactness alone looks like a weak relationship. Split by height, it is two flat clouds. The pattern was height all along.
narration: |
  Compactness alone looks like a weak positive relationship with heating load. Colour
  the points by height, and the picture splits into two flat clouds: the tall buildings
  up here, the low buildings down there, and within each cloud, compactness barely
  matters. The pattern was height all along. This is why E D A is a loop. The first
  plot sends you to a better question, and the better question is: what else changes
  when compactness changes?
:::

:::scene{type=cards}
heading: Which plot for which question
items:
  - title: Histogram
    text: One column. How is it distributed? Skew, clusters, outliers.
    tag: shape
  - title: Scatter
    text: Two columns. Do they move together? Colour by a third before believing it.
    tag: relationship
  - title: Box plot
    text: One column across groups. Do the groups differ, and by how much?
    tag: difference
  - title: Pair plot
    text: Every column against every other. The overview, in one grid.
    tag: overview
narration: |
  Which plot for which question. A histogram: one column, how is it distributed?
  Skew, clusters, outliers. A scatter: two columns, do they move together? Colour by a
  third before you believe the shape. A box plot: one column across groups, do the
  groups differ, and by how much? And a pair plot: every column against every other,
  the whole dataset in one grid, which is where most people start the loop.
:::

:::scene{type=code}
heading: seaborn — statistical plots in one line
code: |
  import seaborn as sns

  sns.boxplot(data=df, x="glazing_area", y="heating_load")
  sns.pairplot(df[["relative_compactness", "overall_height",
                   "glazing_area", "heating_load"]])
  sns.heatmap(df.corr(numeric_only=True), annot=True, cmap="coolwarm")
note: seaborn sits on top of matplotlib and knows about DataFrames. Give it the table and the column names; it draws the comparison, the grid, or the correlation matrix.
narration: |
  Seaborn sits on top of matplotlib and knows about DataFrames. Give it the table and
  the column names, and it draws the comparison, the grid of panels, or the correlation
  matrix with sensible defaults. A box plot of heating load by glazing area, in one
  line. A pair plot of four columns, in one line. A heat map of every correlation, in
  one line. On the website, seaborn installs itself from a copy shipped with the course
  the first time you run the step.
:::
