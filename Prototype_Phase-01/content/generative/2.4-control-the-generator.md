## Generation Control Lab

How can we compare settings without confusing their effects?

Write a prediction, inspect the outputs, and explain what changed before moving to the next experiment.

Compare settings **within each experiment**. The seed, guidance, negative-prompt, inference-step and resolution studies use different architectural prompts; differences across these groups cannot be attributed to one setting.

Use the images to inspect composition, light, openings and architectural detail. Judge each change against the design question, not visual polish alone.

:::reflect{feedback="Guidance controls the strength of conditioning; the seed changes the sampling initialization."}
Why can the same prompt produce different outputs when the seed changes?
- The seed changes how strongly the model follows the prompt
- *The seed changes the randomness used in sampling
:::

:::details{summary="Show reference answer"}
A prompt is a condition, not a complete image specification. Seed-dependent sampling can produce different members of the distribution. For repeatability, record the model/version, scheduler, prompt, seed and all generation settings; a seed alone is insufficient.
:::

## Interpret a controlled experiment

{{Guidance}} and a {{negative prompt}} offer steering, not guarantees. Keep a record of what you changed and what you held fixed. Compare more than one seed when investigating whether an apparent effect is consistent.

:::reflect{feedback="Prompt adherence is one criterion; spatial coherence and architectural requirements also matter."}
A high-guidance image looks closer to your prompt. How should you judge its architectural usefulness?
- Rank it highest because matching the brief visually is the main design criterion
- *Assess the intended visual qualities alongside architectural limitations
:::

:::details{summary="Show reference answer"}
Prompt adherence is only one criterion. A strongly emphasized façade pattern may reduce spatial coherence, and a convincing rendering still lacks verified assemblies and systems. Choose criteria that follow from the design question.
:::

:::details{summary="Why change one input at a time?"}
If both material and seed change, you cannot separate their contributions to the observed differences. Hold the seed and other settings fixed to compare material terms. Even then, changing one input can affect several visual properties: a prompt term is not an independent geometric parameter.
:::

## The control hierarchy

Everything in the lab above lives on the first two rungs of a ladder: words, and the
settings of the sampler. They are the cheapest controls and the weakest. Above them
sit three more, each of which imposes more and suggests less — and only the last one
can be checked.

:::figure{id=control_hierarchy caption="Figure 1. Five levels of control over a generative process. Each rung imposes more and suggests less. A generated image only becomes a checkable design at the last one, which lives outside the generator."}
:::

:::narration
Everything in the lab lives on the first two rungs of a ladder: the words of the
prompt, and the settings of the sampler. They are the cheapest controls and the
weakest. Reference conditioning gives the model a picture to follow instead of a
phrase to imagine. Structural control imposes where things go, with edges, masks,
layouts or poses fed through a control network. And explicit design constraints,
dimensions, adjacencies, code, physics, sit outside the generator altogether. They are
the only rung that can be checked, and reaching it is what lesson two point six is
about.
:::

:::cards
Prompt control | words: intent, vocabulary, hints | steers a distribution; guarantees nothing (Lesson 2.3)
Settings & stochastic control | seed, guidance, steps, negative prompt, resolution | repeatable runs and stronger adherence; still no geometry (this lesson)
Reference conditioning | an image, a sketch, a style or a depth map as an input | the output follows a given picture, not just a phrase; image-to-image, style reference
Structural control | edges, masks, layouts, poses through a control network | where things go is imposed, not suggested; ControlNet-style conditioning, inpainting masks
Explicit design constraints | dimensions, adjacencies, code, physics — outside the generator | the only rung that can be verified, against a structured model (Lesson 2.6)
:::

:::reflect{feedback="A reference or a structural control fixes what the image follows; a dimension is still not verified until the output is a checkable model."}
You feed the generator your own massing sketch as a reference and a plan outline as a structural control. The output now follows both closely. Which of these has been established?
- The building in the image has the floor area of your plan outline
- *The image follows your outline; the area is still a claim to verify in a model built from it
- The generated façade will meet the daylight target you wrote in the prompt
:::

:::details{summary="Show reference answer"}
Reference and structural control move you up the hierarchy: the image now follows a given picture and a given layout rather than a phrase. That is a real gain in control. But an image that follows an outline still has no dimensions, levels or assemblies — those exist only once the output is translated into a structured representation and checked against explicit constraints, the last rung, which Lesson 2.6 walks through.
:::

:::keyidea
**More control means more work and more that can be verified.** The hierarchy is not a
ranking of good and bad tools; a prompt is the right rung for exploring, and a
constraint is the right rung for deciding. Knowing which rung you are on is what
keeps an exploration from being mistaken for a decision.
:::
