:::scene{type=idea icon=spark}
text: Generative AI learns patterns from data / and makes new things that fit them.
sub: Text, images, code, geometry, design representations — conditioned on prompts, examples, references, context, or constraints.
narration: |
  Here is the definition the whole module rests on, in plain words. Generative A I is a
  model that learns patterns from data and can generate new content or structured
  outputs: text, images, code, geometry, or design representations, conditioned on
  prompts, examples, references, context, or constraints. Models learn regularities
  from examples. The output could be an image, a paragraph, a script or a room list.
  Inputs such as prompts and reference images guide the output.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=bulb}
text: To generate is to sample. / Each run draws one possibility.
sub: Ask for "a timber community library in Seattle" and you do not get the design. You get one image among many that fit the words. The same prompt can produce different outputs across multiple generations.
narration: |
  What does generate actually mean? It means to sample. The model has learned a whole
  space of possibilities from its data, and each run draws one member of that family
  under the condition you gave it. Ask for a timber community library in Seattle, and
  you do not get the design. You get one image among many that fit those words. The same
  prompt can produce different outputs across multiple generations. Each output
  needs to be assessed against the design brief.
:::

:::scene{type=cards}
heading: Five ways to steer a generator
items:
  - title: Prompt
    text: '"a timber library, soft overcast light" — words. Steers, guarantees nothing.'
    tag: weakest
  - title: Examples
    text: Three plans of the kind you mean. The model imitates the format and manner.
  - title: Reference
    text: A sketch, a site photo, a depth map. The output follows a picture, not a phrase.
  - title: Constraints
    text: Dimensions, adjacencies, code, physics — checked outside the generator.
    tag: strongest
narration: |
  The definition listed five kinds of condition, and they are not equally strong. A
  prompt communicates intent in words: it steers, but guarantees nothing. Examples show
  the model the kind of output you want, and it imitates the format. A reference, a
  sketch or a site photo, gives it something to follow rather than imagine. Context
  supplies relevant facts. Constraints specify requirements such as dimensions or
  performance limits. Prompt adherence can be assessed, but architectural constraints
  require explicit checks, usually outside the generator. Lesson two point four arranges these into a ladder.
:::

:::scene{type=diagram id=gen_families}
heading: Four ways to learn a distribution
narration: |
  Four approaches account for most of what is in use, and they differ in how the
  model learns and samples. A generative adversarial network, a GAN, trains two networks
  against each other: a generator that makes samples and a discriminator that tries to
  tell them from real ones. A variational autoencoder compresses examples into a small
  latent space and decodes new points from it. A diffusion model learns to remove noise,
  and generates by denoising step by step: that is what makes most of today's images.
  An autoregressive model predicts the next token, one piece at a time: language models
  work this way. And multimodal systems are not a fifth approach. They combine encoders
  and generators so text and images can be inputs and outputs of one system.
:::

  How is generative A I actually used in architecture? A systematic review from twenty
  twenty five read one hundred and sixty one journal papers and asked four questions.
  Which models: GANs lead the published work, with transformers and diffusion models
  rising since twenty twenty one. Which design phase: sixty nine percent of studies sit
  in the schematic phase, and later phases are underexplored. Which data: images
  dominate, both in and out. And how is good judged: mostly by comparison, or by the
  authors themselves; only about one study in six used a third party. Keep that last
  finding in mind. In this field, good usually means judged good by the people who made
  it.
:::

  possibilities, fast. A material, an atmosphere, a massing idea. A draft brief, a
  first script, variations on a reference. What still needs your judgment is everything
  that would let a design decision rest on the output: which question to ask, what to
  hold fixed, whether the image is buildable, the dimensions, structure, access and
  performance, and the translation into a model you can analyse. Generative A I widens
  what you can consider. It does not narrow what you can defend.
:::

:::scene{type=steps}
heading: The arc of this module
steps:
  - Prompt — a condition
  - Generate — sample a possibility
  - Control — change one thing at a time
  - Compare — what actually changed?
  - Evaluate — what can the image establish?
  - "*Translate — into something you can analyse"
narration: |
  The rest of the module follows one experiment through this arc. Prompt: write a
  condition. Generate: sample a possibility. Control: change one thing at a time and see
  what it does. Compare: what actually changed? Evaluate: what can the image establish,
  and what can it not? And translate: turn the picture into something you can analyse
  before you claim anything about how the building performs.
:::
