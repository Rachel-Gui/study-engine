## Central question

**Central question:** when an AI output enters a design, who is responsible for what it
becomes — and what does the output actually establish?

Every module in this course ends with a boundary: what a result does and does not
establish. This lesson is that boundary applied to the people around the tool. It
draws on two recent reviews — a systematic review of ethics in AI and robotics across
the AEC industry (Liang et al., 2024) and a review of human–AI collaboration in
conceptual design (Fang et al., 2025) — and on what the generative-AI review of
Lesson 2.1 found about how outputs are evaluated (Jang, Roh & Lee, 2025).

:::narration
When an A I output enters a design, who is responsible for what it becomes, and what
does the output actually establish? Every module in this course ends with a boundary:
what a result does and does not establish. This lesson is that boundary applied to the
people around the tool. It draws on two recent reviews: one of ethics in A I and
robotics across the architecture, engineering and construction industry, and one of
how designers and generative A I collaborate in conceptual design. And it keeps one
sentence from lesson two point one in view: a plausible output is not evidence.
:::

:::keyidea
Three questions to ask of any AI contribution to a design: **Where did this come from**
(data, model, prompt, settings)? **Who decided** to keep it, and on what check? **What
would it take** to turn it into evidence a client, a reviewer or a building inspector
would accept?
:::

## Nine ethical issues, as the AEC literature names them

Liang et al. (2024) reviewed 314 papers on AI and robotics in AEC (2017 to early 2022)
through the lens of ethics and identified nine recurring issues. They are worth
learning by name, because they are the vocabulary reviewers and clients will use.

:::figure{id=ethics_map caption="Figure 1. The nine ethical issues Liang et al. (2024) identify in AEC uses of AI and robotics, grouped here by what they concern, and the seven research directions the review recommends. Counts are the number of reviewed papers raising each issue. Original diagram after the review's classification."}
:::

:::cards
About data | data transparency · data privacy · data security | opaque models built by third parties; workers and buildings captured without consent; models tampered with in shared clouds
About people | acceptance and trust · job loss · fear of surveillance | tools deployed without the practitioners who must trust them; monitoring that changes behaviour
About decisions | decision-making conflict · reliability and safety · liability | when the tool and the professional disagree; no common criteria to validate robustness; who answers for a learned system's error
:::

:::narration
Liang and colleagues reviewed three hundred and fourteen papers on A I and robotics in
the built environment and found nine ethical issues that keep recurring. Three are
about data: transparency, because most tools are black boxes built by third parties
without the practitioners who use them; privacy; and security. Three are about people:
acceptance and trust, job loss, and the fear of being watched. And three are about
decisions: conflict when the tool and the professional disagree, reliability and
safety, and liability, which is the question of who answers when a learned system is
wrong. Data transparency was the most frequently raised issue of the nine.
:::

Two things about the scope of that review matter for this course. First, its corpus
predates generative AI: authorship, attribution, hallucination and copyright — the
issues a studio meets first — are not among the nine, and the review lists **bias and
fairness**, **explainable AI** and a **responsibility framework** as directions for
future work rather than as findings. Second, only 14 of the 314 papers had ethics as
their subject; the rest touched it incidentally. The field has been building tools
faster than it has been examining them.

:::keyidea
The nine issues are the AEC industry's own list. The generative-AI issues this course
adds — authorship, attribution, hallucination, copyright, over-reliance — are the
newer layer on top of it, not a replacement for it.
:::

## Where bias enters

A model is biased when its outputs systematically favour some cases over others in a
way the task does not justify. In architecture the bias rarely arrives as a single
decision; it accumulates through the workflow.

:::workflow
Training data | Which buildings, cities, climates and styles are in the corpus — and which are not. A facade classifier trained on one city's streets knows that city. | 
The prompt | "Contemporary", "iconic", "clean" carry assumptions about whose architecture counts as the default. A prompt is a small dataset of preferences. | 
The evaluation | Jang, Roh & Lee (2025) found that most generative-design studies judged their outputs by comparison and by the authors' own assessment; third-party assessment was used in 17.4 %. A model judged good by the people who built it has not been tested for who it fails. | 
The deployment | A tool built for schematic imagery used to make a decision about a real site, a real budget, or a real community. | 
:::

:::narration
Where does bias enter? Rarely as one decision. It accumulates. In the training data:
which buildings, cities, climates and styles are in the corpus, and which are not. In
the prompt: words like contemporary or iconic carry assumptions about whose
architecture counts as the default. In the evaluation: the review of a hundred and
sixty one generative design papers found that most outputs were judged by comparison
and by their own authors, and only about one in six by a third party. And in the
deployment: a tool built for early imagery used to make a decision about a real site
and a real community.
:::

**Read the training data on the model card.** The model behind this module's images
says what it learned from. Its Hugging Face card (Lesson 2.2) states that Stable
Diffusion v1.5 was fine-tuned on 512 × 512 images from *LAION-Aesthetics v2 5+*:
image–text pairs collected from the web and kept because an automatic scorer rated
them as aesthetically pleasing. Which buildings, places and styles that filter keeps,
and which it drops, is the question this section asks.

:::screenshot{src=assets/generative/real/hf-sd15-training.png full=assets/generative/real/hf-sd15.png caption="From the model card of Stable Diffusion v1.5: initialised from v1.2, then fine-tuned for 595,000 steps on 512 × 512 images from LAION-Aesthetics v2 5+. A card does not list which cities or architectures are in the data; it tells you where to start asking."}
:::

:::predict
A facade-material classifier scores 94 % on street-view images from the city it was
trained on. The office proposes using it to survey a city on another continent. What is
the first question?
- Whether 94 % is high enough | The number describes the training city; it says nothing yet about the new one.
- *What the training data covers — and what it has never seen | Yes. Coverage is where bias enters. The right next step is a labelled sample from the new city, evaluated before use.
- Whether the model can be made deeper | Capacity is not the problem; the sample is.
:::

## Authorship, attribution, and agency

Fang et al. (2025) reviewed 54 studies of generative AI in conceptual design and
organised them along three dimensions: the **stage** of conceptual design, the
**collaboration mode** between designer and tool, and the **interaction medium** (text,
sketch, image). Their central observation is that generative AI, at the conceptual
stage, mainly widens options — it "fosters creative expression and stimulates
divergent thinking". Choosing, verifying and committing remain with the designer.

:::figure{id=human_ai_collab caption="Figure 2. Human–AI collaboration in conceptual design. Generative AI widens the options; the designer frames, judges, selects and signs. The three dimensions — stages, collaboration modes, interaction media — follow Fang et al. (2025); the responsibility line is the course's own addition."}
:::

:::narration
Who is the author? A review of fifty four studies on generative A I in conceptual
design found that the tool's main contribution is to widen the options: it fosters
creative expression and divergent thinking. Choosing, verifying and committing stay
with the designer. That is the practical answer to authorship. The tool proposes; the
person who selects, edits, checks and signs is the author, and with authorship comes
the responsibility. A generated image in a submission is not anonymous. Someone chose
it, and that someone answers for it.
:::

Attribution is the record of that responsibility. The course's own generative
experiments in Lessons 2.2 to 2.7 ship with a `provenance.json` that records the
model, its version, the prompt, the seed and every setting for every image — because
without that record a result cannot be repeated, questioned, or defended. The same
standard applies to your own work:

:::cards
Which tool, which version | model name and version, or the service and date | "Stable Diffusion v1.5", not "AI"
What it was given | the prompt, the reference image, the settings, the seed | the conditions decide the family of outputs
What a person did to it | selection among how many candidates, edits, translation to geometry | the human contribution, stated
What was checked | dimensions, structure, code, performance — and by what | the difference between an image and a claim
What remains unverified | plainly listed | the reviewer's first question, answered before it is asked
:::

Copyright and licensing sit next to attribution and are unsettled: training corpora,
the ownership of outputs, and the reuse of published figures are governed differently
in different jurisdictions and are changing. Lystbæk (2025) lists copyright and data
privacy among the open concerns of generative tools in building design. This course
takes the conservative position: cite the source of anything you use, redraw rather
than copy a published figure unless its licence permits reuse, and state when a
result was generated.

## Human oversight and appropriate reliance

Oversight is not a checkbox that says a human was present. It is a decision, made
each time, about how much to rely on a tool for this task — and the evidence says
people get that decision wrong in both directions.

:::compare
Over-reliance | accepting fluent output without a check | Qiao, Shihab & Hundhausen (2026): in one study, 93 % of AI code explanations contained something correct and 50 % contained something incorrect — at the same time. Faster does not mean understood; over-dependence "might diminish [the] ability to comprehend code independently".
Under-reliance | rejecting a validated tool because it is a tool | Liang et al. (2024): decision-making conflict and low acceptance follow when tools arrive without the practitioners who must trust them. A validated surrogate that is never used has cost more than it saved.
:::

**Appropriate reliance** means calibrating trust to what has actually been validated:
the task, the data range, the reported error and its conditions. The Reference section
(9.1) is the course's checklist for that calibration; the habits below are its
day-to-day form.

:::cards
Trust it for | tasks inside its validated range, with a stated error, where a wrong answer is cheap or caught downstream | a draft, a first sketch, a triage signal
Verify before use | anything a decision will rest on: dimensions, code, structure, energy, cost | run the check the output implies
Do not use it for | tasks outside its data range, tasks with no way to check, decisions that need a licensed professional's judgment | the model has nothing to say here
:::

:::narration
Oversight is not a box that says a human was present. It is a decision, made each
time, about how much to rely on the tool for this task, and people get it wrong in
both directions. Over reliance: in one study, ninety three percent of A I code
explanations contained something correct, and fifty percent contained something
incorrect, at the same time. Under reliance: a validated tool that nobody trusts has
cost more than it saved. Appropriate reliance is trust calibrated to what has been
validated: the task, the data range, the reported error, and its conditions.
:::

## AI output versus validated architectural evidence

The distinction this course keeps returning to. Each kind of output has a specific
route to becoming evidence, and none of the routes runs through the generator.

:::compare
AI-generated output | an image, a paragraph, a label, a number | plausible; produced from patterns; unverified by construction; the reasons behind it are usually not inspectable
Validated architectural evidence | a measurement, a simulation with stated inputs, a checked drawing, a model evaluated on unseen cases with its error and range reported | reproducible; traceable to its inputs; its failure conditions are known
:::

:::workflow
A rendering | establishes a possibility — a material, an atmosphere | becomes evidence only after translation to geometry (Lesson 2.6) and analysis with stated inputs | 
A language-model answer | establishes a draft | becomes evidence only when its claims are checked against the source — the code clause, the standard, the measurement | 
A classifier's label | establishes a triage signal | becomes evidence only after inspection, and only inside the population it was validated on | 
A prediction | establishes an estimate | becomes evidence only with its error on unseen data, its data range and its known failures (Modules 6–8) | 
:::

:::predict
A language model drafts the code-compliance section of a design report and cites two
clauses of the building code. Before the section goes to the client, what is it?
- Evidence — the model read the code | The model produced text that resembles a citation. Clause numbers are among the things it gets wrong most fluently.
- *A draft whose two citations must be checked against the actual code text by a person who is accountable for the report | Yes. The check is the work; the draft only saved the typing.
- Evidence if the model is a recent one | Recency changes the error rate, not the category.
:::

:::narration
The distinction the whole course keeps returning to. An A I output is plausible,
produced from patterns, and unverified by construction. Validated architectural
evidence is reproducible, traceable to its inputs, and its failure conditions are
known. Each kind of output has a route to becoming evidence, and none of the routes
runs through the generator. A rendering becomes evidence after translation to geometry
and analysis. A language model's answer becomes evidence when its claims are checked
against the source. A classifier's label becomes evidence after inspection. A
prediction becomes evidence with its error on unseen data and its range.
:::

## Responsibility in practice: the AI use statement

For every studio submission and every report in this course that used an AI tool,
attach a short statement. It is the professional habit in five lines, and it answers
the questions a reviewer would otherwise have to ask.

:::cards
1 · Tools | which model or service, which version, on which date | 
2 · Use | what it was used for — imagery, drafting, code, prediction — and at which stage | 
3 · Inputs | the prompts, references and settings, or where they are recorded | 
4 · Checks | what was verified, how, and by whom | 
5 · Limits | what remains unverified, and what the output must not be read as | 
:::

**An example, for this module's own images.** The statement for Figure 1 of Lesson 2.1,
the four generated library images, written from the record that ships with them:

```
AI USE STATEMENT · Lesson 2.1, Figure 1 (four generated library images)

1 Tools   Stable Diffusion v1.5, model id runwayml/stable-diffusion-v1-5 (text-encoder
          snapshot 451f4fe…; other component versions not recorded), diffusers
          StableDiffusionPipeline, Google Colab GPU (T4 reported), course notebook, 2026.
2 Use     Teaching imagery at the concept stage: one prompt yields a family of outputs.
3 Inputs  Prompt "A community library with exposed timber structure, large glass facade,
          located on a sloped urban site in Seattle, soft overcast morning light,
          contemporary Scandinavian style, architectural photography, 8K"; seeds 100,
          200, 300, 400; guidance 7.5; 30 steps; no negative prompt. All recorded in
          real-experiments/provenance.json.
4 Checks  The images are the notebook's saved outputs, cropped without resampling; their
          hashes are recorded. Nothing architectural was checked: no structure,
          dimensions, access or performance.
5 Limits  Not designs, not proposals for a real site, not evidence of buildability or
          performance. Do not read the glazing, the structure or the scale as specified.
```

Notice what it does with the unknowns: it names them ("not recorded", "nothing
architectural was checked") instead of leaving them out. A statement that admits a gap
is more useful to a reviewer than one that looks complete.

Liang et al. (2024) end their review by calling for a **responsibility framework** —
because, as they put it, the decision-making process becomes related "not only to the
decision-maker but also to the AI system creation process". Until such a framework
exists, the licensed professional remains the accountable party, and the statement
above is how that accountability is made visible.

:::boundary
Data | two systematic reviews (Liang et al., 2024, 314 AEC papers, 2017 – early 2022; Fang et al., 2025, 54 design studies) and the generative-AI review of Lesson 2.1 (Jang, Roh & Lee, 2025, 161 papers) — [[retrieved]], with their scopes stated
Evidence | the nine issues, the seven directions, the collaboration dimensions and the evaluation percentages are the reviews' findings; the AI use statement and the reliance table are the course's own practice, not a published standard
Establishes | a vocabulary for discussing bias, responsibility and evidence, and a checklist for attribution and reliance
Does not establish | legal advice on liability or copyright in any jurisdiction, a validated measure of bias in any tool, or a substitute for professional judgment and licensing requirements
:::

:::keyidea
**A plausible output is not evidence, and a tool is not an author.** The person who
selects, checks and signs is responsible for what an AI contribution becomes — and the
AI use statement is how that responsibility is made visible.
:::
