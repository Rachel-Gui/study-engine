## What generative AI is

**Central question:** how can generative AI help architects explore design
possibilities while maintaining meaningful control and architectural judgment?

:::keyidea
**Generative AI** refers to models that learn patterns and representations from data
and can generate new content or structured outputs — such as text, images, code,
geometry, or design representations — conditioned on prompts, examples, references,
context, or constraints.
:::

Generative models learn regularities from examples and use them to produce outputs
such as images, paragraphs, scripts, meshes or room adjacency graphs. Inputs such as
prompts and reference images guide the output. This module examines how much control
those inputs provide and what still needs to be verified.

:::narration
Generative A I refers to models that learn patterns and representations from data,
and can generate new content or structured outputs, such as text, images, code,
geometry or design representations, conditioned on prompts, examples, references,
context or constraints. Models learn regularities from examples. The product may be
an image, but it may equally be a paragraph, a script, a mesh or an adjacency graph.
And what you supply steers what comes out, with a strength that ranges from a hint to
a hard constraint. A convincing architectural image is a starting point for inquiry.
It can suggest a material, an atmosphere, a relationship to a site. It does not tell
us whether the structure works or how much energy the building needs. This module
follows an experiment from its first condition through generation, control,
comparison, critique and translation, so that possibilities become useful without
confusing appearance with evidence.
:::

Here is what that looks like. Four images, one prompt — *a community library with
exposed timber structure, large glass facade, on a sloped urban site in Seattle, soft
overcast morning light* — generated in the course notebook with Stable Diffusion
v1.5. Only the random seed changed between them.

Look at them as an architect would. Each is plausible: a roof, a glazed wall, timber
that reads as timber. None of them is a design. There is no plan behind any of them,
no structure has been checked, no site has been surveyed, and the fourth differs from
the first in ways the prompt never asked for. That is what *sampling under a
condition* means in practice — and why this module spends as long on judgment as on
generation.

**Why this matters.** Prompting expresses design intent, constraints, style and
performance hints in language. Generative AI can produce possibilities rapidly, but a
convincing image is not the same as a valid architectural decision. This module is
about learning to control, compare, critique and translate outputs into architectural
workflows.

:::flow
PROMPT / CONDITION > GENERATE > CONTROL > COMPARE > EVALUATE > TRANSLATE
:::

Throughout the arc, **predict → interact → observe → explain → reflect**. The final
activity brings your decisions together in one design experiment record.

:::details{summary="Orientation note · predictive and generative tasks"}
Modules 6 to 8 are about **predictive** models: given a building's characteristics, X, estimate an outcome, ŷ — an energy use intensity, a class, a load. A generative model does something different in kind: given a condition, it produces a possible new artifact. The two are teaching roles rather than exclusive technologies — a language model generates text through repeated next-token predictions — but the questions you ask of them differ. Of a prediction: how close is it to the measured outcome? Of a generation: is this possibility useful, and what would make it valid?
:::

## What does “generate” mean?

A {{generative model}} learns patterns in data and samples new outputs resembling
those patterns. Think of a {{distribution}} of possibilities rather than a database
returning one exact design. To generate is to **sample** from that distribution under
a {{condition}}: each run draws one member of a family the model has learned. The same prompt can produce different outputs across multiple generations.

:::cards
Learned from data | patterns and representations, not rules | the model has seen many libraries; it has not been told what a library is
Sampled, not retrieved | each output is new and one of many | no output is "the" answer to the prompt
Conditioned | steered by what you supply | a prompt, an example, a reference, a context, a constraint
Any representation | text, image, code, geometry, a design representation | an image alone is not reliable geometry
:::

:::reflect{feedback="A condition steers a distribution of possibilities rather than specifying one uniquely correct design."}
Given “a timber community library in Seattle,” what might a text-to-image generative model produce?
- The single design that best satisfies the brief
- *One possible image among many alternatives consistent with the condition
:::

:::details{summary="Show reference answer"}
The condition steers sampling from learned patterns. It does not specify one uniquely correct image, and the output is not a simulation result. Text, images, layouts and geometry are different possible output representations; an image alone is not reliable geometry.
:::

:::narration
To generate is to sample. The model has learned a distribution of possibilities from
its data, and each run draws one member of that family under the condition you gave.
The same prompt can produce different outputs across multiple generations. That is why a prompt for a timber library in
Seattle does not return the design that best satisfies the brief. It returns one
possible image among many that are consistent with the condition, and another run
may return a different one.
:::

## How is generation conditioned or controlled?

The definition lists five kinds of condition, and they are not equally strong. A
**prompt** communicates intent in words. **Examples** show the model the kind of output
you want. A **reference** — an image, a sketch, a depth map — gives it something to
follow rather than something to imagine. **Context** supplies relevant facts. **Constraints** specify requirements such as
dimensions or performance limits. Prompt adherence can be assessed, but architectural
constraints require explicit checks, usually outside the generator.

:::cards
Prompt | "a timber community library in Seattle, soft overcast light" | steers; guarantees nothing (Lesson 2.3)
Examples | three plans of the kind you mean | the model imitates the format and the manner
Reference | a sketch, a massing model, a photograph of the site | the output follows a given picture, not just a phrase (Lesson 2.4)
Context | the brief, the site area, the climate | facts the model can use instead of inventing (Lesson 2.8)
Constraints | dimensions, adjacencies, code, physics | imposed and verified outside the generator (Lesson 2.6)
:::

Lesson 2.4 arranges these into a **control hierarchy** — prompt control, settings and
stochastic control, reference conditioning, structural control, explicit design
constraints — and the labs walk up it one rung at a time.

:::reflect{feedback="Words steer a distribution; a checkable constraint is imposed and verified outside the generator."}
Which kind of condition can a generated design be *held to*?
- A prompt that says "two storeys, 800 m², south-facing reading room"
- *An explicit constraint checked against a structured representation of the output
- A negative prompt that says "no cantilevers"
:::

:::details{summary="Show reference answer"}
A prompt and a negative prompt change what is likely, not what is possible: the model can and does produce a three-storey image from a two-storey prompt. Only when the output has been translated into something structured — dimensions, levels, adjacencies — can a constraint be checked, and the check happens outside the generator.
:::

## What major generative model approaches exist?

Four approaches account for most of what is in use. They differ in *how* the
distribution is learned and sampled, and the difference decides what is easy to
control and what is not.

:::figure{id=gen_families caption="Figure 2. Four generative model approaches, and multimodal systems, which are not a fifth approach but a way of combining encoders and generators so that several modalities can be inputs and outputs of one system."}
:::

The literature agrees on the families and on which are rising. A systematic review of
161 journal papers on generative AI in architectural design (Jang, Roh & Lee, 2025)
finds GANs leading the published work, with transformers and diffusion models gaining
since 2021 because they "reflect user intent in real time"; convolutional and
feed-forward networks dominate the earlier papers. The same review measures how fast
the field moves: the gap between an architecture's introduction in AI research and its
first architectural application fell from 62 years to 2.5 — a 96 % reduction.

:::narration
Four approaches account for most of what is in use. A generative adversarial network
trains two networks against each other: a generator that makes samples and a
discriminator that tries to tell them from real ones. A variational autoencoder
compresses examples into a latent space and decodes samples and smooth variations
from it. A diffusion model learns to remove noise under a condition, and generates by
denoising, step by step. An autoregressive model predicts the next token, and language
models work this way. Multimodal systems are not a fifth approach. They combine
encoders and generators so that text, images and other modalities can be the inputs
and outputs of one system. The approach decides what is easy to control and what is
not.
:::

Open each approach to compare its basic idea, typical outputs and possible
architectural roles.

:::details{summary="GAN · generator and discriminator"}
**Idea:** during training, a generator makes samples while a discriminator distinguishes real examples from generated ones. The discriminator is not a structural engineer or an architectural quality assessor.
**Typical output:** images or plan-like layouts.
**Architectural application:** House-GAN / House-GAN++ generate floor-plan layouts conditioned on room adjacency. Similar-looking plans still require circulation and dimensional checks.
:::

:::details{summary="VAE · explore a latent space"}
**Idea:** encode examples into a compressed probabilistic latent space, then decode samples and variations.
**Typical output:** reconstructed images or new variations.
**Architectural application:** interpolate layout or massing concepts. A smooth latent transition does not guarantee a valid design transition.
:::

:::details{summary="Diffusion · iterative denoising"}
**Idea:** learn to remove noise under a condition, then iteratively transform noise into a sample.
**Typical output:** images; other representations depend on the particular model.
**Architectural application:** explore material, atmosphere and façade concepts. Diffusion supports the image-generation experiments in this module (Lessons 2.2 to 2.7).
:::

:::details{summary="Autoregressive / transformer · predict the next token"}
**Idea:** represent the output as a sequence of tokens — words, code, or tokenised geometry — and learn to predict each token from the ones before it. A transformer is the network architecture that makes this work over long contexts.
**Typical output:** text, code, structured data such as JSON, and sequence-encoded geometry such as a room schedule or a polyline.
**Architectural application:** draft a brief, convert a description into a structured design representation, write a script. Large language models are autoregressive models over text; Lesson 2.8 is about them. A fluent output still needs verification.
:::

**Multimodal systems** are treated separately because they are *systems* rather than a
learning approach: a text encoder, an image encoder and one or more generators
combined so that an image can be described in words, a sketch can condition an image,
or a brief can become a layout. A multimodal model can describe a rendering; whether
its description is accurate is a different question, and Lesson 2.5 asks it.

:::details{summary="Multimodal systems · capabilities across modalities"}
**Idea:** combine encoders and generators so that several kinds of input — text, images, sometimes audio, geometry or documents — can condition several kinds of output within one system.
**Typical capability:** caption an image, answer questions about it, generate an image from text and a reference sketch, produce text and structured data from a drawing.
**Architectural application:** discuss a precedent image, turn a sketch and a brief into a candidate rendering, extract a room list from a plan. Each capability inherits the limits of the approaches it combines.
:::

:::reflect{feedback="Resemblance and adjacency constraints do not establish all architectural requirements."}
A generated floor plan resembles real plans and matches requested room adjacencies. What should you check next?
- Treat the matched adjacencies as sufficient evidence that circulation works
- *Check dimensions, circulation and structural requirements explicitly
- Use visual similarity to real plans as the main test of buildability
:::

:::details{summary="Show reference answer"}
The learning objective and representation determine what a model can constrain. Visual resemblance, discrimination, or denoising is not equivalent to checking a design against explicit architectural requirements.
:::

## What can generative AI contribute, and what still needs judgment?

Before the answer, the evidence on how generative AI is actually being used in
architecture. The review of 161 papers by Jang, Roh & Lee (2025) asked four questions
— which models, which design phases, which data, which evaluation — and the answers
set the expectations for this module.

:::figure{id=genai_review_map caption="Figure 3. What a systematic review of 161 papers on generative AI in architectural design (2014–2024) found, on its four questions. Generative AI is used overwhelmingly in the schematic phase (68.94 %); images dominate inputs and outputs; and evaluation is mostly comparative or the authors' own judgement. Original diagram after Jang, Roh & Lee (2025); the percentages are the review's."}
:::

Three findings matter here. Generative AI is a **schematic-phase** tool in the
literature: 68.94 % of studies apply it there, and later phases — where accountability
and licensing arise — remain underexplored. Its outputs are mostly **images**
(68.32 %), which is why Lesson 2.6 exists: an image has to be translated before
anything can be analysed. And its **evaluation** is weak: comparative evaluation
(60.9 %) and the authors' own assessment (34.2 %) dominate, third-party assessment is
used in 17.4 % of studies, and 105 different metrics are in circulation. "Good" in a
paper usually means "judged better than a baseline by the people who made it". A
review of human–AI collaboration in conceptual design (Fang et al., 2025) draws the
same line from the other side: generative AI at the conceptual stage mainly "fosters
creative expression and stimulates divergent thinking" — it widens options; choosing
and verifying stay with the designer.

:::compare
What generation contributes | many possibilities, fast; a material, an atmosphere, a massing idea; a draft brief; a first script; variations on a reference | breadth at the start of a process, and a way to make intent visible and comparable
What still requires judgment and verification | which question to ask; what to hold fixed; whether an image is buildable; dimensions, structure, access, performance; the translation into a model that can be analysed | every claim that would let a design decision rest on an output
:::

:::narration
What does generation contribute? Breadth at the start of a process: many
possibilities, fast, a material, an atmosphere, a massing idea, a draft brief, a first
script. And a way to make intent visible and comparable. What still requires judgment
and verification is everything that would let a design decision rest on an output.
Which question to ask. What to hold fixed. Whether the image is buildable. Dimensions,
structure, access and performance. And the translation of a picture into a model that
can be analysed. The rest of this module is the practice of both halves.
:::

The lessons that follow practise both halves. 2.2 shows how a diffusion model turns
noise into an image under a condition. 2.3 and 2.4 put you in control of the
generator and show where control runs out. 2.5 asks what a convincing image can and
cannot establish. 2.6 translates an image into information a simulation can use. 2.7
brings it together in one recorded experiment. 2.8 turns to language models and
structured output, the bridge to Modules 3 and 4, and 2.9 puts a model on your own
laptop.

:::keyidea
Generative AI widens what you can consider. It does not narrow what you can defend.
The output of every generator in this module is a hypothesis; the checks that follow
are what make it a claim.
:::
