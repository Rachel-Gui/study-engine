:::scene{type=title}
narration: |
  Welcome to lesson two point ten, critical A I in architecture: bias, responsibility,
  and evidence. Every lesson so far ended with a boundary: what a result does and does
  not establish. This lesson is that boundary applied to the people around the tool.
  When an A I output enters a design, who is responsible for what it becomes? And what
  does the output actually establish? Two recent reviews, one of ethics across the
  building industry and one of how designers work with generative A I, give the
  vocabulary.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=question}
text: Three questions / for any AI contribution to a design.
sub: Where did this come from? Who decided to keep it, and on what check? What would it take to turn it into evidence a client, a reviewer or an inspector would accept?
narration: |
  Three questions, and you can ask them of any A I contribution to a design. Where did
  this come from: which data, which model, which prompt, which settings? Who decided to
  keep it, and on the strength of what check? And what would it take to turn it into
  evidence that a client, a reviewer, or a building inspector would accept? Everything
  in this lesson is a way of answering one of those three.
:::

:::scene{type=diagram id=ethics_map}
heading: Nine ethical issues, as the building industry names them
points:
  - About data — transparency, privacy, security
  - About people — trust, job loss, surveillance
  - About decisions — conflict, reliability, liability
  - Seven directions, including bias and a responsibility framework
narration: |
  A systematic review by Liang and colleagues read three hundred and fourteen papers on
  A I and robotics in architecture, engineering and construction, and found nine
  ethical issues that keep coming back. Three about data: transparency, privacy,
  security. Three about people: acceptance and trust, job loss, and the fear of being
  watched. Three about decisions: conflict when the tool and the professional disagree,
  reliability and safety, and liability. Data transparency was raised most often. And
  the review closes with seven directions, among them bias and fairness, explainable A
  I, and a framework for responsibility, which it lists as work still to be done.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=warn}
text: That list predates generative AI.
sub: Authorship, attribution, hallucination, copyright, over-reliance — the issues a studio meets first — are the newer layer on top of the nine, not a replacement for them.
narration: |
  One thing to notice about that list. The papers it reviewed stop in early twenty
  twenty two, before generative A I reached every desk. So the issues a studio meets
  first, authorship, attribution, hallucination, copyright, and over reliance, are not
  among the nine. They are the newer layer on top of the industry's list, not a
  replacement for it. This lesson covers both layers.
:::

:::scene{type=steps}
heading: Where bias enters — it accumulates
steps:
  - Training data — which buildings, cities and styles are in the corpus
  - The prompt — "contemporary", "iconic" carry assumptions
  - "*The evaluation — judged by comparison and by the authors"
  - The deployment — a schematic tool used for a real decision
narration: |
  Bias rarely arrives as a single decision. It accumulates along the workflow. In the
  training data: which buildings, which cities, which climates and styles are in the
  corpus, and which are not. In the prompt: words like contemporary or iconic carry
  assumptions about whose architecture counts as the default. In the evaluation: the
  review of a hundred and sixty one generative design papers found that most outputs
  were judged by comparison and by their own authors, and only about one in six by a
  third party. And in the deployment, when a tool built for early imagery is used to
  make a decision about a real site and a real community.
:::

:::scene{type=diagram id=human_ai_collab}
heading: Who is the author?
points:
  - Generative AI widens the options — divergent thinking
  - The designer frames, judges, selects and signs
  - Media from words to structured data — the further right, the more you can check
narration: |
  Who is the author? A review of fifty four studies on generative A I in conceptual
  design found that the tool's main contribution is to widen the options: creative
  expression, divergent thinking. Choosing, verifying and committing stay with the
  designer. That is the practical answer. The tool proposes; the person who selects,
  edits, checks and signs is the author, and with authorship comes responsibility. The
  media run from words, which enforce nothing, to structured data, which can be
  checked. The further right you work, the more of the output you can verify, and the
  more you are committing to.
:::

:::scene{type=compare}
heading: Reliance — people get it wrong in both directions
left:
  title: Over-reliance
  points:
    - Accepting fluent output without a check
    - In one study, 93 % of AI code explanations had something right and 50 % had something wrong — at once
    - Faster is not understood; dependence erodes the skill
right:
  title: Under-reliance
  points:
    - Rejecting a validated tool because it is a tool
    - Decision conflict and low trust follow when tools arrive without the people who must use them
    - A validated surrogate nobody uses cost more than it saved
verdict: Appropriate reliance is trust calibrated to what has actually been validated — the task, the data range, the reported error and its conditions.
narration: |
  Oversight is not a box that says a human was present. It is a decision, made each
  time, about how much to rely on the tool for this task, and people get it wrong in
  both directions. Over reliance: in one study, ninety three percent of A I explanations
  of code contained something correct, and fifty percent contained something incorrect,
  at the same time. Faster is not the same as understood. Under reliance: a validated
  tool that nobody trusts has cost more than it saved. Appropriate reliance is trust
  calibrated to what has actually been validated: the task, the data range, the
  reported error, and its conditions.
:::

:::scene{type=cards}
heading: AI output is not evidence — until
items:
  - title: A rendering
    text: A possibility. Evidence only after translation to geometry and analysis with stated inputs.
    tag: Lesson 2.6
  - title: A language-model answer
    text: A draft. Evidence only when its claims are checked against the source — the clause, the standard, the measurement.
    tag: Lesson 2.8
  - title: A classifier's label
    text: A triage signal. Evidence only after inspection, and only inside the population it was validated on.
    tag: Lesson 8.1
  - title: A prediction
    text: An estimate. Evidence only with its error on unseen data, its data range and its known failures.
    tag: Modules 6–8
narration: |
  The distinction the whole course keeps returning to. An A I output is plausible,
  produced from patterns, and unverified by construction. Evidence is reproducible,
  traceable to its inputs, with failure conditions that are known. Each kind of output
  has a route to becoming evidence, and none of the routes runs through the generator.
  A rendering becomes evidence after translation to geometry and analysis. A language
  model's answer, when its claims are checked against the source. A classifier's label,
  after inspection. A prediction, with its error on unseen data and its range.
:::

:::scene{type=list}
heading: The AI use statement — five lines on every submission
items:
  - Tools — which model or service, which version, which date
  - Use — what it did, at which stage
  - Inputs — prompts, references, settings, or where they are recorded
  - Checks — what was verified, how, and by whom
  - Limits — what remains unverified, and what the output must not be read as
narration: |
  Responsibility in practice is five lines attached to every submission that used an A
  I tool. Tools: which model or service, which version, which date. Use: what it did,
  and at which stage. Inputs: the prompts, references and settings, or where they are
  recorded. Checks: what was verified, how, and by whom. Limits: what remains unverified,
  and what the output must not be read as. Until the industry has the responsibility
  framework the review calls for, the licensed professional remains the accountable
  party, and this statement is how that accountability is made visible.
:::
