  Welcome to lesson two point five: can we trust the image? You will look at a
  convincing A I generated library, a long upper volume floating over a glazed ground
  floor, broad stairs, soft daylight, and learn to ask it the questions an architect
  would ask a student. What is visible, what is inferred, and what cannot be known from
  this picture at all.

:::scene{type=idea icon=eye}
text: Separate what you see, / what you infer, / and what you cannot know.
sub: An observation, an inference, and an unknown are three different kinds of statement. A critique that keeps them apart is one you can act on.
narration: |
  The whole lesson is one habit. When you look at a generated image, separate three
  things. What you see: there is a projecting upper volume. What you infer: it is
  probably a reading room. And what you cannot know from this view: whether anything
  holds it up. An observation, an inference and an unknown are three different kinds of
  statement. A critique that keeps them apart is one you can act on.
:::

:::scene{type=cards}
heading: Five places to look
items:
  - title: The cantilever
    text: The upper volume projects past the glass. Its support is not visible. Ask for sections and spans.
    tag: structure
  - title: Stairs and access
    text: A broad stair dominates; a lower entrance appears at the side. Where is the accessible route?
    tag: circulation
  - title: Window rhythm
    text: Openings change size and spacing. Intentional? The image says nothing about the rooms behind.
    tag: façade
  - title: Levels
    text: The slope and partial views make floor counting ambiguous. Sections, not bands.
    tag: geometry
narration: |
  Five places to look in the library image. The cantilever: the upper volume projects
  past the glass, and its support is not visible. Ask for sections and spans; do not
  conclude it is impossible, and do not conclude it is fine. Stairs and access: a broad
  stair dominates, a lower entrance appears at the side, and the accessible route is
  nowhere. The window rhythm: openings change size and spacing, and the image says
  nothing about the rooms behind them. The levels: the sloping site makes floor counting
  ambiguous. And the fifth, performance: soft daylight and timber cladding suggest an
  atmosphere, and establish nothing about energy or materials.
:::

:::scene{type=quiz}
question: Which conclusion about the projecting upper volume is best supported by the image?
options:
  - A hidden steel frame is probably enough, so move on to other checks
  - The projection is too long, so the structural concept should be rejected
  - Its structural strategy needs verification beyond this image
answer: 2
pause: 5
explain: The image gives a clue about a projection — not member sizes, materials, support conditions or loads. A useful critique says what information would resolve the question.
narration: |
  A check. Which conclusion about the projecting upper volume is best supported by the
  image? A: a hidden frame is probably enough, move on. B: the projection is too long,
  reject the concept. C: its structural strategy needs verification beyond this image.
  Pause and pick one.
after: |
  It is C. The image gives you a clue about a projection. It gives you no member sizes,
  no materials, no support conditions, no loads. Both A and B claim to know something
  the picture cannot show. A useful critique names what information would resolve the
  question: a section, a span, a structural strategy.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=warn}
text: A confident caption is not a measurement.
sub: '"This building has three floors." A multimodal model can describe an image fluently. Fluent is not measured.'
narration: |
  Now a second kind of A I. Multimodal models can look at an image and describe it, or
  answer questions about it. Ask how many floors the library has and you may get a
  confident answer: three. But the sloping site and the facade bands admit several
  readings, and the model's confidence is a style of speech, not a measurement. Use a
  caption to suggest questions to ask. Verify quantities against drawings.
:::

:::scene{type=compare}
heading: Plausible or valid?
left:
  title: Visually plausible
  points:
    - Coherent light and shadow
    - Materials that look right
    - A composition you would photograph
    - Learned from thousands of real photos
right:
  title: Architecturally valid
  points:
    - A load path that works
    - An accessible route that connects
    - Levels that align in section
    - Performance you can simulate
verdict: Plausibility is what the model was trained for. Validity is what you are trained for.
narration: |
  The distinction the whole module turns on. Visually plausible: coherent light and
  shadow, materials that look right, a composition you would photograph. The model was
  trained on thousands of real photographs, so plausibility is exactly what it is good
  at. Architecturally valid: a load path that works, an accessible route that connects,
  levels that align in section, performance you can simulate. Plausibility is what the
  model was trained for. Validity is what you are trained for.
:::
