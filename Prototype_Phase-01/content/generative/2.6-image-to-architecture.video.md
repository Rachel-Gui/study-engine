  Welcome to lesson two point six, from image to architecture. You have a generated
  image you like. A client asks: how much energy will it use? This lesson is about
  everything that has to happen between that picture and any honest answer, and about
  keeping a record of your experiments so someone else could repeat them.

:::scene{type=idea icon=bulb}
text: A generated image is a starting point, / not a result.
sub: It proposes a massing, a material, an atmosphere. An image alone does not provide reliable dimensions, material properties or operating conditions for simulation.
narration: |
  Start here. A generated image is a starting point, not a result. It is a visual
  hypothesis about a possible building: it proposes a massing, a material, an
  atmosphere. An image alone does not provide reliable dimensions, material properties
  or operating conditions for simulation. So between the
  image and the claim sits a chain of translation, and every link in it is work a person
  does and can get wrong.
:::

:::scene{type=diagram id=image_to_model}
heading: From a picture to a claim
narration: |
  Here is the chain. The generated image: pixels, a visual hypothesis. Extract: walls,
  glazing, levels, labelled by you and corrected by you, with a scale. Structured
  information: dimensions, orientation, assemblies, and a list of what is still
  unknown. A geometry model: parametric or B I M, checked. Simulate: with weather,
  schedules and systems. And only then, a claim, with a range and an uncertainty. Notice
  the dashed line underneath. No performance claim can jump that gap. The image is the
  start. The checked model is what gets simulated.
:::

:::scene{type=cards}
heading: What is missing before an energy evaluation
items:
  - title: Geometry
    text: Dimensions, levels, orientation. Pixels have none of them.
  - title: Assemblies
    text: What the walls, roof and glazing are made of, and their thermal properties.
  - title: Operation
    text: Who is inside, when, and what the heating and cooling systems are.
  - title: Weather
    text: A climate file for the real site — not the sky in the picture.
narration: |
  What is actually missing before you could run an energy evaluation? Geometry:
  dimensions, levels, orientation. Pixels have none of them, even when they look
  precise. Assemblies: what the walls, roof and glazing are made of, and their thermal
  properties. Operation: who is inside, when, and what the heating and cooling systems
  are. And weather: a climate file for the real site, not the sky in the picture. Even a
  facade that is visibly shaded in one image tells you nothing about its annual
  performance.
:::

:::scene{type=quiz}

narration: |
  A check. What is the essential bridge between a generated image and an architectural
  evaluation? A: refine the image until it is completely unambiguous. B: translate it
  into structured design information, and verify that information. C: generate more
  images from more angles. Pause and pick.
after: |
  It is B. More imagery, sharper or from more angles, widens the exploration, but it
  never supplies the missing inputs. Structured, checked information is what makes a
  design available to parametric tools, B I M, and simulation. That translation is
  designer's work, and it is where the real decisions get made.
:::

:::scene{type=code}
heading: Code for reproducible experiments
code: |
  settings = dict(prompt="A timber community library in Seattle",
                  seed=100, guidance=7.5, steps=30, negative_prompt="")
  previous = settings.copy()
  current = {**settings, "seed": 200}

  changed = {k: (previous[k], current[k])
             for k in previous if previous[k] != current[k]}
  print("Changed:", changed)
output: |
  Changed: {'seed': (100, 200)}
note: Ten lines that answer the question every reviewer asks — what exactly did you change between these two images? A seed alone is not a record; the model, its version and every setting belong in it.
narration: |
  Now the second half of the lesson: keeping a record. Generative design is a pipeline,
  not a single act, and code can make the pipeline reproducible. Here the settings of an
  experiment sit in a small dictionary: prompt, seed, guidance, steps, negative prompt.
  Copy them, change the seed, and a few lines compute exactly what differs. Changed:
  seed, one hundred to two hundred. That answers the question every reviewer asks:
  what exactly did you change between these two images? And notice: a seed alone is not
  a record. The model and every setting belong in it.
:::

narration: |
  A little context from the research. In twenty twenty five, Jang, Roh and Lee reviewed
  one hundred and sixty one published studies of generative A I in architectural design
  from the previous decade. Most of them sit in early design: concept, layout,
  rendering. Later phases and construction documentation are far less developed. And
  images dominate as inputs and outputs, because drawings are easier to exchange than
  rich geometry and system information. Evaluation, they found, is fragmented: different
  studies judge different things, and visual similarity is not spatial validity.
:::
