:::scene{type=title}
narration: |
  Welcome to lesson two point three, prompting as design. A prompt is the place where
  your intent becomes language. This lesson is a controlled experiment: you take the
  prompt for a small pavilion, change exactly one word, the material, and watch what
  happens to the image. Then we ask the harder question: what does a prompt actually
  guarantee? The answer is, less than it seems.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=bulb}
text: Change one thing. / Hold everything else fixed.
sub: Same prompt, same seed, same settings. Only the material word changes — timber, concrete, steel and glass, rammed earth.
narration: |
  The method first, because it is the method that makes this a lesson and not a
  slideshow. Change one thing, and hold everything else fixed. Same prompt, same seed,
  same guidance, same steps. Only the material word changes: timber, concrete, steel
  and glass, rammed earth. If two things change at once, you cannot tell which one
  caused what you see. Scientists call this a controlled experiment. Designers should
  too.
:::

  Architectural photography: the kind of picture. And one variable, primary material

:::scene{type=cards}
heading: The three parts of an architectural prompt
items:
  - title: Design intent
    text: Community use, a calm reading environment. What the proposal should support.
    tag: purpose
  - title: Visual vocabulary
    text: Material, context, lighting, style. What makes the image legible and comparable.
    tag: look
  - title: Performance hint
    text: Passive daylighting, south-facing shading. An intention that still needs geometry and simulation.
    tag: hypothesis
narration: |
  A good architectural prompt has three parts. The design intent: community use, a calm
  reading environment, what the proposal should support. The visual vocabulary:
  material, context, lighting, style, the words that make the image legible and
  comparable. And a performance hint: passive daylighting, south facing shading. That
  last part is the one to be careful with. It is an intention, and the image will show
  you a look. The look is not the performance.
:::

:::scene{type=code}
heading: The experiment in code
code: |
  BASE = ("A small pavilion in a public garden, open structure, "
          "elegant proportions, surrounded by trees, soft afternoon "
          "light, architectural photography")
  for material in ["timber", "concrete", "steel and glass", "rammed earth"]:
      g = torch.Generator("cuda").manual_seed(200)   # same seed, every time
      image = pipe(f"{BASE}, primary material is {material}",
                   num_inference_steps=30, guidance_scale=7.5,
                   generator=g).images[0]
output: |
  four images · the material term is the only input that differs
note: Simplified from the script that made the four images. Everything is written down; the generator is reset to seed 200 before every image.
narration: |
  This is what a controlled comparison looks like in code. The fixed part of the prompt
  is written down once. A loop runs over the four materials. Before every image the
  random generator is reset to the same seed, two hundred, so every image starts from
  the same noise. Steps and guidance never change. If two images differ, the material
  word is the only input that differs. That still does not tell you which visual change
  the word caused. It tells you nothing else was changed.
:::

narration: |
  Here is what happens in the experiment. You ask for one change: timber to concrete.
  What actually changes? The material, yes. But also the roof, from a flat plane to a
  rounded form. The supports, from slender posts to broad piers. The openings, the
  viewpoint, the whole composition. One changed input, several changed qualities.
  The model learned that concrete pavilions tend to look a certain way, and it brought
  the whole look along. Prompt terms are not independent design variables.
:::

:::scene{type=idea icon=warn}
text: Prompts communicate intent. / They do not guarantee constraints.
sub: A phrase influences several correlated visual patterns at once. Wording is not a substitute for a dimension, a rule, or a check.
narration: |
  This is the idea of the lesson, and it is worth saying slowly. Prompts communicate
  intent. They do not guarantee constraints. A phrase influences several correlated
  visual patterns at once, and you cannot pin one of them down with words. Wording is
  not a substitute for a dimension, a rule, or a check. If you need the overhang to be
  one point two metres, no prompt will make it so. Only a model you can measure will.
:::

  A check. A prompt says south facing shading, and the image shows a deep overhang.
  What has been established? A: the overhang is correctly sized for a south facade. B: a
  possible visual interpretation of the phrase, which still needs checking. C: that the
  building faces south. Pause and pick one.
after: |
  It is B. The image may show fins or an overhang, and they may look right. It does not
  establish which way is north, how deep the overhang is, how it is built, or what it
  does over a year. Translate the idea into explicit geometry, and then evaluate it.
:::

:::scene{type=list}
heading: Make the brief specific
items:
  - '"Design a library" — too open for anyone to assess'
  - A neighbourhood in Seattle — a real site with a climate and a street
  - Reading spaces, community use, an area budget — things you can check for
  - Check the proposed room areas — do they add up, and do they fit?
narration: |
  A practical habit, whether the brief is written by you or drafted by a language
  model. Design a library is too open for anyone to assess. A brief that names a
  Seattle neighbourhood, reading spaces, community use, a site boundary and an area
  budget can be assessed, because it makes claims you can check. And when a model
  drafts room areas for you, add them up. Fluent briefs are as unchecked as fluent
  images.
:::
