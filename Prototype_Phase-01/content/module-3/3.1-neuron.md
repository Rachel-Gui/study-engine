## Where neural networks came from

:::narration
A little history first, because it explains why neural networks work the way they do. The
idea is older than the computer you are running it on. In nineteen forty three,
McCulloch and Pitts described a neuron as a device that sums its inputs and fires if
the sum crosses a threshold. In nineteen fifty eight, Rosenblatt built the perceptron,
a machine that could learn those weights from examples instead of having them set by
hand. It could only draw straight boundaries, and interest in the field collapsed.
What revived it was backpropagation, in nineteen eighty six: an efficient way to work
out how every weight in a stack of layers contributed to the error. Now notice the gap
between nineteen eighty six and twenty twelve. Almost nothing changed mathematically.
What changed was the amount of data available and the arrival of G P Us. Hold on to
that, because when a network of yours underperforms, the answer is usually more or
better data, not a cleverer architecture.
:::

:::figure{id=timeline caption="Figure 1. Five moments that produced the modern neural network. Redrawn for this module."}
:::

:::deck{dir=assets/lec-ann label="Lecture slides — deep learning" count=0}
:::

The perceptron could only draw straight boundaries, and interest collapsed. What
revived the field was **backpropagation** (Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams, 1986): an
efficient way to work out how every weight in a *stack* of layers contributed to the
error, which made deep networks trainable in practice.

:::keyidea
Almost nothing changed mathematically between 1986 and 2012. What changed was the
volume of available data and the arrival of GPUs. When a network underperforms, the
answer is usually more or better data, not a cleverer architecture.
:::

## Artificial neuron

:::narration
Now the building block. An artificial neuron is a mathematical function. It is not a
realistic simulation of a biological neuron, and the analogy is worth holding loosely.
One neuron does exactly two things. First it takes a weighted sum of its inputs and
adds a bias. Then it passes that single number through an activation function, which
we write as phi. That is the entire unit. The weights say how much each input matters.
The bias shifts the threshold at which the neuron responds. And training, all of
training, is just searching for values of the weights and the bias that make the
network's outputs close to the truth. In building terms: if your inputs are kitchen
temperature, humidity and hour of the day, the weights answer the question, how much
does each of those matter for predicting load. The network discovers those numbers
from data, rather than you supplying them.
:::

An artificial neuron is a mathematical function, **not** a realistic simulation of a
biological neuron.

:::figure{id=neuron caption="Figure 2. Inputs, weights, bias, summation, activation, output. Redrawn for this module."}
:::

An artificial neuron contains:

- **Inputs** — building area, height, window-to-wall ratio, orientation, materials, weather, or other {{features}}
- **{{Weights}}** — values learned during training that set the relative influence of each input
- **{{Bias}}** — a learned value that shifts the neuron's response
- **Weighted sum** — the combination of inputs and weights
- **{{Activation function}}** — a transformation that introduces a nonlinear response
- **Output** — a value passed to the next layer, or used as the final prediction

:::keyidea
z = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + wₙxₙ + b, then a = φ(z). The weights and the bias are learned
from data. The activation φ is chosen by you.
:::

## Interactive mini code lab — run an artificial neuron

:::narration
Here is that same neuron in three lines of numpy. Change the inputs, the weights or
the bias, and press run. Two things are worth trying. Set every weight to zero and
watch the output collapse to whatever the bias allows. Then make the weighted sum
negative and watch ReLU clip it to zero. That clipping is the entire nonlinearity, and
it is what the next section is about.
:::

Change the inputs, the weights or the bias, then run the cell. Everything executes in
your browser — nothing is uploaded, and nothing is installed.

:::pylab{title="One neuron, three lines" packages=numpy}
# step: Compute one neuron
import numpy as np

def neuron(x, w, b, phi=lambda z: np.maximum(0, z)):
    z = np.dot(w, x) + b       # weighted sum + bias
    return phi(z)              # activation

x = np.array([21.5, 44.0, 19.0])     # temperature, humidity, hour
w = np.array([ 0.8, -0.2,  1.4])     # learned weights
b = -3.0                             # learned bias

print("z =", np.dot(w, x) + b)
print("a =", neuron(x, w, b))

# step: Try to kill the nonlinearity
# Stack two layers with NO activation and check what you actually built.
W1, W2 = np.random.randn(4, 3), np.random.randn(2, 4)
two_layers = W2 @ (W1 @ x)
one_matrix = (W2 @ W1) @ x
print("identical:", np.allclose(two_layers, one_matrix))
:::

## Common activation functions

:::narration
This is the idea that makes the whole thing work, and you just proved it in the lab.
Stack two layers with no activation between them, and W two times W one x is the same
as W two W one, times x. Two layers collapse into a single matrix. A hundred layers
would do the same. So without a nonlinear phi, depth buys you absolutely nothing; you
have written a very expensive linear regression. ReLU, the maximum of zero and z, is
the default choice. It costs one comparison, and its gradient is exactly one for
positive inputs, so the error signal survives being passed back through many layers.
Sigmoid and tanh squash everything into a fixed range and their gradients fall to
nearly zero at the extremes, which stalls learning in deep stacks. The one exception
is the output layer. For regression it stays linear, because the answer is an
unbounded number. Put a sigmoid there and you have capped your prediction at one.
:::

:::figure{id=activations caption="Figure 3. Three activations, and the collapse that happens without one. Redrawn for this module."}
:::

- **{{ReLU}}** — commonly used in hidden layers; passes positive values, suppresses negative ones
- **Sigmoid** — maps output between 0 and 1; useful for binary probabilities
- **Softmax** — converts several class scores into multiclass probabilities
- **Linear output** — useful for continuous {{regression}} targets
- **Tanh** — maps output between −1 and 1; historically common in sequence models

:::widget{id=activation-lab}
:::

:::cards
ReLU | max(0, z) | hidden layers, default
Sigmoid | 1 / (1 + e⁻ᶻ) | binary probabilities
Softmax | scores → probabilities | multiclass output
Linear | z | regression output
:::

:::keyidea
W₂(W₁x) = (W₂W₁)x. Without a nonlinear activation, any stack of layers is one linear
map. Depth only buys you something once φ is nonlinear.
:::

## Network layers, shallow and deep

:::narration
Stack neurons side by side and you have a layer. Stack layers and you have a network.
Each layer takes the previous layer's outputs as its own inputs, so one forward pass is
just a sequence of matrix multiplies with an activation after each one. Width is how
many neurons sit in a layer. Depth is how many layers there are. And here is the point
I want to be careful about, because it is easy to get wrong. A deeper network is not
automatically a better network. On the appliance-energy dataset in episode three point
seven, a four-layer network of five hundred and twelve, two hundred fifty six, one
twenty eight and sixty four neurons scored worse than a two-layer network of sixty
four and thirty two. Greater capacity raises the requirement for data, computation,
tuning and validation. It does not raise accuracy on its own.
:::

:::figure{id=network caption="Figure 4. Input layer, two hidden layers, one linear output. Redrawn for this module."}
:::

- **Input layer** — receives building parameters, climate, images, graphs, sensors, or simulation data
- **Hidden layers** — learn intermediate representations and interactions
- **Output layer** — produces a prediction, class, segmentation, or physical field

A **shallow** network has one or two hidden layers and suits relatively simple,
structured problems. A **deep** network has several and can learn increasingly abstract
representations.

:::widget{id=capacity-lab}
:::

:::keyidea
A deeper network is not automatically a better network. Measured on the Episode 3.7
dataset, identical training: 64-32 reached R² 0.380, while 512-256-128-64 reached
0.353. Greater capacity raises the requirement for data, computation, tuning and
validation — not the accuracy.
:::

:::compare
Shallow | One or two hidden layers | Fewer parameters, faster to train, easier to explain to a reviewer, and often all a structured tabular problem needs.
Deep | Several hidden layers | More abstract representations, at the cost of more data, more tuning, more compute, and more ways to fail quietly.
:::

## What this episode does and does not establish

:::narration
Finally, the boundary. Everything in this episode describes how a network computes,
not what any particular network is entitled to claim. The figures use the appliance
energy dataset from episode three point seven: a single occupied house near Mons in
Belgium, ten-minute readings across four and a half months from January to May, one
temperate maritime climate, one occupancy pattern. There is no cooling season in that
window. A model fitted there does not transfer to another building, another climate or
another year, and the accuracy figures quoted are for that data alone. State the range
your own model was fitted on, every time you report a number from it.
:::

Every figure in this episode is drawn from the Episode 3.7 dataset. Its range is the
range of any claim made from it.

:::stats
1 house | occupied, low-energy, near Mons, Belgium
19,735 | ten-minute readings
Jan–May | 4.5 months, no cooling season
:::

- **Geometry range** — one dwelling; no variation in form, envelope or orientation
- **Climate range** — temperate maritime; a single heating-dominated period
- **Metrics** — R² and MAE on a held-out split; no uncertainty interval is reported
- **Known failure conditions** — another building, another climate, another year, or a
  summer cooling load. A random train/test split on ten-minute data also flatters the
  score, because adjacent rows are near-duplicates; Episode 3.3 shows what a
  chronological split does to it.

:::keyidea
A network learns patterns in its training data. It does not establish causation,
guarantee code compliance, or replace a licensed professional's judgment.
:::

:::details{summary="Explore the data dictionary"}
The dataset carries 28 measured channels plus a timestamp. T1–T9 and RH_1–RH_9 are
temperature and relative humidity in nine rooms, sampled by a ZigBee network every
ten minutes. T_out, RH_out, Press_mm_hg, Windspeed, Visibility and Tdewpoint come
from the weather station at Chièvres airport. Appliances and lights are the two
energy meters, in watt-hours per interval. rv1 and rv2 are random numbers the
authors planted deliberately, to test whether a model can identify a feature that
carries no signal.
:::
