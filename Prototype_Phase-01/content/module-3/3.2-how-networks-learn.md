## Central question

When a neural network makes a wrong prediction, how does it work out which weights
to change?

:::narration
A network starts with random weights, so its first prediction is meaningless. The
question this episode answers is how it gets from there to something useful. Not by
being told the right weights, but by measuring how wrong it is and adjusting. Four
steps, repeated: predict, measure the error, work out each weight's share of the
blame, and nudge every weight a little way against it. That third step is
backpropagation, and it is the reason deep networks are practical at all.
:::

## Complete training loop

1. Split data into training, {{validation set}}, and {{test set}}.
2. Forward pass: inputs move through the network and produce a prediction.
3. Compute {{loss function}}: compare the prediction with the known target.
4. {{Backpropagation}}: calculate how each weight contributed to the error.
5. Optimization: update weights to reduce the loss.
6. Validation: measure performance on data not used for weight updates.
7. Repeat across batches and epochs.
8. Evaluate once on the untouched test set.

:::keyidea
Steps 2 to 5 are one update. Steps 2 to 7 repeated over the whole dataset is one
{{epoch}}. Step 8 happens once, ever.
:::

## Key terms

- **Parameter** — a weight or {{bias}} learned from data.
- **{{Hyperparameter}}** — a setting selected or searched by the researcher: layer
  count, neuron count, {{learning rate}}, {{batch}} size, optimizer, dropout rate,
  or epoch count.
- **Batch** — a subset of training examples used for one update.
- **{{Epoch}}** — one complete pass through the training dataset.
- **Learning rate** — the size of each weight update.
- **Loss function** — the training objective that the model minimizes.
- **Optimizer** — the update strategy, such as gradient descent, SGD, or Adam.

:::details{summary="Why Adam is the usual default"}
Plain gradient descent applies one learning rate to every weight. Adam
(Kingma & Ba, 2015) keeps a running estimate of the gradient and its variance for
each individual weight and adapts the step size accordingly, which is why
`Adam(1e-3)` works across so many problems without tuning. It is a good default,
not a guarantee — if training diverges, the learning rate is the first thing to
lower.
:::

## Loss examples

- **{{Regression}}** — mean squared error or mean absolute error.
- **{{Classification}}** — binary or categorical cross-entropy.
- **Physics-informed learning** — data loss + equation or physics loss + boundary or
  initial-condition loss.

## Architectural interpretation

:::keyidea
A model does not understand energy automatically. It learns the relationship
encouraged by the data, the representation, the loss function, and the constraints
selected by the researcher.
:::

That sentence is the whole responsibility of the person building it. If the loss
only rewards average accuracy, the model will be bad at peaks and will not tell you
so. If the representation has no occupancy signal, no amount of training invents one.
