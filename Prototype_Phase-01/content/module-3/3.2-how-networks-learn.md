## Central question

When a neural network makes a wrong prediction, how does it work out which weights
to change?

:::narration
A network starts with random weights, so its first prediction is meaningless. The
question this episode answers is how it gets from there to something useful. Not by
being told the right weights, but by measuring how wrong it is and adjusting. Four
steps, repeated: predict, measure the error, work out each weight's share of the
blame, and nudge every weight a little way against it. That third step is
backpropagation, and it is the reason deep networks are practical at all. By the end
you will have written it yourself, in about fifteen lines of numpy.
:::

:::figure{id=training_loop caption="Figure 1. The training loop. Redrawn for this module."}
:::

:::flow
Predict > Measure the loss > Backpropagate the blame > Update every weight > Repeat
:::

By the end of this episode you should be able to:

- Trace one training update through a two-layer network by hand
- Explain what the learning rate does, and recognise the three ways it goes wrong
- Say why features are standardised before training, in terms of the loss surface
- Read a loss curve and know whether training has finished, stalled, or diverged

## Complete training loop

:::narration
Here is the whole loop, and I want you to notice how little of it is mysterious. Split
the data three ways first, and set the test set aside. Then, for every batch: run the
inputs forward to get a prediction, compare it with the truth to get a loss, run the
error backwards to get a gradient for every weight, and step every weight a little
way downhill. After each pass through the data, check the loss on the validation set,
which the weights have never touched. That number decides when to stop. The test set
is used exactly once, at the end, to report a score you did not tune towards.
:::

1. Split data into training, {{validation set}}, and {{test set}}.
2. Forward pass: inputs move through the network and produce a prediction.
3. Compute {{loss function}}: compare the prediction with the known target.
4. {{Backpropagation}}: calculate how each weight contributed to the error.
5. Optimization: update weights to reduce the loss.
6. Validation: measure performance on data not used for weight updates.
7. Repeat across batches and epochs.
8. Evaluate once on the untouched test set.

:::keyidea
Steps 2 to 5 are one update. Steps 2 to 7 repeated over the whole dataset is one
{{epoch}}. Step 8 happens once, ever.
:::

:::cards
Forward pass | inputs → prediction | a chain of matrix multiplies and activations
Loss | prediction vs truth | one number: how wrong, on average
Backward pass | loss → gradient of every weight | the chain rule, applied in reverse
Update | w ← w − η · ∂L/∂w | every weight moves a little downhill
:::

## Loss: the number the network minimises

:::narration
Everything the network learns, it learns through the loss, so the loss is the most
consequential choice you make. For regression the usual choice is mean squared error:
the average of the squared differences between prediction and truth. Squaring
punishes large errors much more than small ones, which is what you want if peaks
matter, and what you do not want if your sensors occasionally glitch. Mean absolute
error is the calmer alternative. For classification the loss is cross-entropy, which
rewards putting probability on the right class. And for physics-informed learning,
the loss is a sum: a data term plus an equation term plus a boundary term, which is
where Episode three point six picks up.
:::

- **{{Regression}}** — mean squared error or mean absolute error.
- **{{Classification}}** — binary or categorical cross-entropy.
- **Physics-informed learning** — data loss + equation or physics loss + boundary or
  initial-condition loss.

:::compare
Mean squared error | (ŷ − y)² averaged | Large errors dominate. Right when peaks matter, wrong when a few readings are glitches.
Mean absolute error | |ŷ − y| averaged | Every error counts equally. More robust to outliers; the gradient does not shrink near the answer.
:::

:::predict
Your appliance-energy model is trained with mean squared error. The sensor log
contains a handful of readings ten times higher than anything real. What happens?
- The model ignores them; they are rare | Rare, but squared. A single error of 1,000 Wh weighs as much as ten thousand errors of 10 Wh.
- *The model bends towards them and its everyday predictions get worse | Yes. MSE makes outliers the loudest voice in the loss. Clean the log, or switch to MAE.
- Training fails with an error message | Nothing fails. The loss is simply optimising the wrong thing, silently.
:::

## Gradient descent and the learning rate

:::narration
Picture the loss as a landscape over all the weights, and the current weights as a
point on it. The gradient is the direction of steepest ascent, so we step the
opposite way, and the learning rate, eta, is the size of that step. Too small and
training crawls; too large and each step overshoots the valley and the loss climbs
instead of falling. There is a third case that matters for building data. If one
feature is measured in thousands and another in fractions, the bowl is stretched into
a long narrow valley, and one learning rate has to serve both directions. Push on the
widget below and you will see why the fix is to standardise the features, not to tune
the learning rate harder.
:::

:::figure{id=loss_landscape caption="Figure 2. Same bowl, same start, three learning rates. Redrawn for this module."}
:::

:::widget{id=gradient-lab}
:::

:::keyidea
**Learning rate** is the size of each weight update. If the loss rises on every step,
lower it. If it falls but crawls, standardise the features first, then raise it. On the
Episode 3.7 data, skipping standardisation cost the same network **R² 0.380 → 0.250**.
:::

## Backpropagation: the chain rule, in reverse

:::narration
Now the step that makes deep networks possible. The loss depends on the output; the
output depends on the last layer's activations; those depend on the weighted sums;
and those depend on the weights. Each of those dependencies is a simple local
derivative. The chain rule says the derivative of the loss with respect to any weight
is the product of the local derivatives along the path back to it. Backpropagation
computes those products for every weight in one backward sweep, reusing the shared
factors, which is why it costs about the same as a forward pass. It was published in
nineteen eighty six, and it is the same algorithm in every framework today.
:::

:::figure{id=backprop_chain caption="Figure 4. One path through a two-layer network, forward on top, backward underneath. Redrawn for this module."}
:::

:::keyidea
∂L/∂w = ∂L/∂ŷ · ∂ŷ/∂a · ∂a/∂z · ∂z/∂w. Every factor is local. Backpropagation is the
bookkeeping that computes every such product in one sweep, sharing the factors.
:::

:::technical{summary="The four gradients of a two-layer regression network"}
With hidden activations a₁ = ReLU(XW₁ + b₁), output ŷ = a₁W₂ + b₂ and loss
L = mean(ŷ − y)²:

```
d_yhat = 2 (yhat - y) / n          # dL/dyhat
dW2    = a1.T @ d_yhat             # dL/dW2
db2    = sum(d_yhat)
d_a1   = d_yhat @ W2.T             # dL/da1
d_z1   = d_a1 * (z1 > 0)           # ReLU: gradient 1 where z > 0, else 0
dW1    = X.T @ d_z1                # dL/dW1
db1    = sum(d_z1, axis=0)
```

That is the entire backward pass for this network. Frameworks like PyTorch derive
the equivalent automatically for any architecture (automatic differentiation), which
is what makes the PINN in Episode 3.9 practical: they can differentiate the network's
*output with respect to its input* too.
:::

## Interactive code lab — backpropagation from scratch

:::narration
Time to write it. The lab builds a two-layer network on a small synthetic problem: two
inputs, window-to-wall ratio and insulation thickness, and one output, heating load,
generated by a rule with an interaction the network has to discover. You write the
forward pass, then the backward pass, then the update, and you watch the loss fall.
Then you try three learning rates on the same network. One crawls, one converges,
one explodes within a few epochs. Those three curves are the most useful picture in
this episode; you will see them again every time you train anything.
:::

Run the steps in order. Everything executes in your browser.

:::pylab{title="Backpropagation from scratch" packages=numpy,matplotlib}
# step: Make a small dataset with a nonlinear rule
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 200 imaginary rooms: window-to-wall ratio and insulation thickness (cm)
wwr = rng.uniform(0.1, 0.8, 200)
ins = rng.uniform(5, 30, 200)
# the "true" heating load, kWh/m2 - a rule with an interaction the model must discover
load = 40 + 90 * wwr**2 - 1.2 * ins + 25 * wwr * (30 - ins) / 25 + rng.normal(0, 3, 200)

X = np.column_stack([wwr, ins])
X = (X - X.mean(0)) / X.std(0)          # standardise the inputs (Episode 3.3 says why)
y = (load - load.mean()) / load.std()   # and the target, so the loss is O(1)
print("inputs", X.shape, " target", y.shape)

# step: Forward pass and loss with random weights
H = 8                                         # hidden neurons
W1 = rng.normal(0, 0.5, (2, H)); b1 = np.zeros(H)
W2 = rng.normal(0, 0.5, (H, 1)); b2 = np.zeros(1)

def forward(X):
    z1 = X @ W1 + b1              # (n, H)   weighted sums
    a1 = np.maximum(0, z1)        # (n, H)   ReLU
    yhat = (a1 @ W2 + b2)[:, 0]   # (n,)     linear output for regression
    return z1, a1, yhat

_, _, yhat = forward(X)
loss = np.mean((yhat - y)**2)
print("loss before training:", round(loss, 3), "(1.0 would be 'predict the mean')")

# step: Backpropagation - the chain rule, written out
def backward(X, y, z1, a1, yhat):
    n = len(y)
    d_yhat = 2 * (yhat - y) / n                 # dL/dyhat
    dW2 = a1.T @ d_yhat[:, None]                # dL/dW2 = a1^T . dL/dyhat
    db2 = d_yhat.sum(keepdims=True)
    d_a1 = d_yhat[:, None] @ W2.T               # dL/da1
    d_z1 = d_a1 * (z1 > 0)                      # ReLU gradient: 1 where z>0, else 0
    dW1 = X.T @ d_z1                            # dL/dW1
    db1 = d_z1.sum(0)
    return dW1, db1, dW2, db2

lr = 0.05
history = []
for epoch in range(300):
    z1, a1, yhat = forward(X)
    loss = np.mean((yhat - y)**2)
    history.append(loss)
    dW1, db1, dW2, db2 = backward(X, y, z1, a1, yhat)
    W1 -= lr * dW1; b1 -= lr * db1; W2 -= lr * dW2; b2 -= lr * db2   # the update
    if epoch % 50 == 0 or epoch == 299:
        print(f"epoch {epoch:3d}  loss {loss:.4f}")
print("R2 on the training data:", round(1 - history[-1], 3))

# step: What a learning rate that is too large does
def train(lr, epochs=120, seed=1):
    global W1, b1, W2, b2
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    W1 = r.normal(0, 0.5, (2, H)); b1 = np.zeros(H); W2 = r.normal(0, 0.5, (H, 1)); b2 = np.zeros(1)
    losses = []
    for epoch in range(epochs):
        z1, a1, yhat = forward(X)
        loss = np.mean((yhat - y)**2)
        if not np.isfinite(loss) or loss > 1e6:
            losses.append(np.nan); break              # diverged: stop, don't overflow
        losses.append(loss)
        dW1, db1, dW2, db2 = backward(X, y, z1, a1, yhat)
        W1 -= lr * dW1; b1 -= lr * db1; W2 -= lr * dW2; b2 -= lr * db2
    return losses

for lr_try in (0.01, 0.05, 1.5):
    losses = train(lr_try)
    last = losses[-1]
    print(f"lr {lr_try:<5} after {len(losses):3d} epochs: " + ("DIVERGED - the loss exploded" if np.isnan(last) else f"loss {last:.3f}"))

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
plt.figure(figsize=(6, 3.2))
for lr_try in (0.01, 0.05, 1.5):
    losses = np.array(train(lr_try), dtype=float)
    plt.plot(np.clip(losses, 0, 3), label=f"lr = {lr_try}")
plt.xlabel("epoch"); plt.ylabel("MSE (standardised)"); plt.legend()
plt.title("Same network, three learning rates (1.5 leaves the chart)")
plt.tight_layout()
:::

:::keyidea
The training R² of 0.98 in step 3 is **not a result**. It was measured on the rows the
weights were fitted to. Episode 3.3 is about the number that would count.
:::

## Optimizers, batches, and epochs

:::narration
Three practical terms. A batch is the subset of rows used for one update; computing
the gradient on thirty-two rows instead of twenty thousand makes each step cheap and
adds a little noise that, in practice, helps. An epoch is one full pass through the
training data. And the optimizer is the rule that turns a gradient into a step. Plain
gradient descent uses one learning rate for everything. Adam keeps a running average
of each weight's gradient and of its variance, and scales every weight's step
individually. That is why Adam with a learning rate of one e minus three is the default
almost everywhere: it is forgiving of badly scaled problems. It is a default, not a
guarantee.
:::

- **Parameter** — a weight or {{bias}} learned from data.
- **{{Hyperparameter}}** — a setting selected or searched by the researcher: layer
  count, neuron count, {{learning rate}}, {{batch}} size, optimizer, dropout rate,
  or epoch count.
- **Batch** — a subset of training examples used for one update.
- **{{Epoch}}** — one complete pass through the training dataset.
- **Learning rate** — the size of each weight update.
- **Loss function** — the training objective that the model minimizes.
- **Optimizer** — the update strategy, such as gradient descent, SGD, or Adam.

:::compare
Full-batch gradient descent | one update per epoch, on all rows | Exact gradient, expensive per step, and it can settle into a poor flat region and stay there.
Mini-batch SGD | one update per 32–256 rows | Cheap, noisy steps. The noise is a feature: it shakes the weights out of shallow minima. The default.
:::

:::cards
SGD | w ← w − η·g | one rate for every weight
Momentum | keeps a velocity | rolls through small bumps, faster along long valleys
Adam | per-weight adaptive steps | the default; Adam(1e-3) works across most problems
:::

:::details{summary="Why Adam is the usual default"}
Plain gradient descent applies one learning rate to every weight. Adam
(Kingma & Ba, 2015) keeps a running estimate of the gradient and its variance for
each individual weight and adapts the step size accordingly, which is why
`Adam(1e-3)` works across so many problems without tuning. It is a good default,
not a guarantee — if training diverges, the learning rate is the first thing to
lower.
:::

:::technical{summary="Adam's update rule, in five lines"}
For each weight, with gradient g at step t and defaults β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, ε = 10⁻⁸:

```
m = b1 * m + (1 - b1) * g          # running mean of the gradient
v = b2 * v + (1 - b2) * g**2       # running mean of its square
m_hat = m / (1 - b1**t)            # bias corrections for the first steps
v_hat = v / (1 - b2**t)
w = w - lr * m_hat / (sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
```

Dividing by √v̂ is what makes the step size per-weight: a weight whose gradient is
consistently large takes proportionally smaller steps. The PINN lab in Episode 3.9
uses exactly these five lines.
:::

## Architectural interpretation

:::narration
One sentence to carry out of this episode. A model does not understand energy. It
learns whatever relationship the data, the representation, the loss and the
constraints encourage it to learn. Every one of those four is chosen by a person. If
the loss only rewards average accuracy, the model will be bad at peaks and will not
tell you. If the representation has no occupancy signal, no amount of training will
invent one. The mathematics in this episode is fixed and shared by everyone. The
choices around it are the design work, and they are yours.
:::

:::keyidea
A model does not understand energy automatically. It learns the relationship
encouraged by the data, the representation, the loss function, and the constraints
selected by the researcher.
:::

That sentence is the whole responsibility of the person building it. If the loss
only rewards average accuracy, the model will be bad at peaks and will not tell you
so. If the representation has no occupancy signal, no amount of training invents one.

:::reflect
Your loss falls smoothly for 40 epochs, then jumps to a huge number and stays there.
What is the most likely cause?
- The dataset is too small | A small dataset overfits; it does not make the loss explode.
- *The learning rate is too large for some part of the loss surface | Yes. Overshooting turns a descent into a climb. Lower the rate, or use Adam, or clip the gradients.
- The network needs more layers | Depth does not repair a diverging optimiser; it usually makes it worse.
:::
