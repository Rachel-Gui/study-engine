## Central question

Why can a model perform extremely well on training data and still fail on a new
building or a new climate?

:::narration
This is the episode that decides whether anything you build is trustworthy. A model
with tens of thousands of parameters and a few thousand training rows can simply
memorise. Memorising drives the training loss towards zero while making the model
useless on anything it has not seen. That is overfitting, and I want to be clear
that it is the default failure mode, not a rare accident. Everything in this episode
is a way of noticing it before somebody else does, and the lab at the end shows the
single most common way building-data papers get it wrong.
:::

:::figure{id=overfitting caption="Figure 1. Training loss always falls. Validation loss is the one that tells the truth. Redrawn for this module."}
:::

:::flow
Split first > Train on one part > Watch the other > Stop at the bottom > Report the third part once
:::

By the end of this episode you should be able to:

- Tell underfitting from overfitting from two numbers
- Choose a split that answers the question you are actually asking
- Use early stopping and weight decay without touching the test set
- Say what a reported R² does and does not establish

## Three model states

:::narration
Two numbers tell you which state a model is in: its error on the training rows, and
its error on rows it has never seen. If both are high, the model is underfitting: it
has not found the pattern that is there, and it needs capacity, features, or time. If
both are low and close together, you have generalisation, which is the only outcome
worth reporting. And if the training error keeps falling while the validation error
flattens or rises, the model has started memorising. Push the capacity slider in the
widget below and watch the two numbers separate; that separation is the whole
phenomenon.
:::

- **{{Underfitting}}** — the model is too simple or insufficiently trained; training
  and validation errors are both high.
- **Good {{generalization}}** — training and validation performance are both
  acceptable and remain close.
- **{{Overfitting}}** — training performance continues to improve while validation
  performance stops improving or becomes worse.

:::figure{id=fit_regimes caption="Figure 2. The same twelve points, three model capacities. Redrawn for this module."}
:::

:::widget{id=fit-lab}
:::

:::compare
Underfitting | Both errors high | Add capacity, add features, or train longer. The model has not yet found the pattern that is there.
Overfitting | Training error low, validation error rising | Add dropout, stop earlier, simplify, or get more data. The model has started memorising.
:::

:::predict
A network scores R² 0.99 on its training rows and 0.61 on a held-out set. A colleague
suggests adding two more layers. What will most likely happen to the held-out score?
- It will rise; more capacity means more accuracy | Capacity is what is already causing the gap. More of it memorises faster.
- *It will stay flat or fall; the model is already overfitting | Yes. The gap says memorising, not underfitting. Regularise, get data, or simplify — do not enlarge.
- It will rise if training runs longer | Longer training widens the gap. The validation curve is already past its minimum.
:::

## Regularization strategies

:::narration
Regularisation is any change that makes memorising harder without making learning
harder. Dropout switches off a random fraction of neurons on every update, so the
network cannot rely on any single one. Weight decay penalises large weights, which
are what a network uses to fit individual points. Early stopping simply stops at the
epoch where the validation loss was lowest and keeps those weights. Data augmentation
makes new training examples by transforming old ones in ways that do not change the
answer. Of all of these, dropout and early stopping are almost free, and they should
be in your default configuration, not your emergency kit.
:::

:::cards
{{Dropout}} | randomly disables some neurons during training | the network cannot depend on any one of them
Weight decay | discourages unnecessarily large weights | large weights are how a network fits single points
{{Early stopping}} | stops when validation stops improving; keeps the best weights | free, and always on
Data augmentation | meaningful variations that do not change the target | more examples from the same data
Batch normalization | stabilises activation distributions layer by layer | steadier training, mild regularisation
{{Cross-validation}} | rotates which slice is held out | a better estimate, not a regulariser as such
Simpler architecture | fewer layers and neurons | the regulariser people forget to try
:::

:::keyidea
Dropout and early stopping are the two cheapest defences and should be your default,
not your last resort (Srivastava et al., 2014).
:::

:::technical{summary="What weight decay and dropout do to the loss, exactly"}
**Weight decay (L2)** adds α·Σw² to the loss, so the gradient of every weight gains a
term 2αw that pulls it towards zero. In scikit-learn's `MLPRegressor` this is the
`alpha` argument; in PyTorch it is `weight_decay` on the optimizer.

**Dropout** multiplies each hidden activation by an independent Bernoulli(1−p) mask
during training and rescales by 1/(1−p), so the expected activation is unchanged. At
prediction time the mask is off. Its effect is close to training an ensemble of
thinned networks and averaging them (Srivastava et al., 2014). scikit-learn's MLP does
not implement dropout; Keras and PyTorch do (`Dropout(0.2)`).
:::

## Interactive code lab — early stopping, weight decay, and leakage

:::narration
Three experiments. First, a network that is far too large for two hundred and forty
rows, trained with no protection: training R squared close to one, test R squared
far below it. Second, the same network with early stopping: it halts after about
fifty epochs, at the point where the validation curve turned, and the test score
improves. Third, a sweep of weight decay judged only on validation. Then the
experiment that matters most for this course: sensor-like data where every reading
resembles the one before it. A random split scores well. A chronological split scores
below zero, worse than predicting the mean. Same data, same model. Only the split
changed.
:::

:::pylab{title="Early stopping, weight decay, and the leakage trap" packages=numpy,scikit-learn,matplotlib}
# step: A small noisy dataset and a network that is too big for it
import numpy as np
from sklearn.neural_network import MLPRegressor
from sklearn.metrics import r2_score
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

n = 240                                            # few rows, like most building datasets
X = rng.uniform(-1, 1, (n, 6))                     # six standardised features
y = np.sin(2 * X[:, 0]) + X[:, 1] * X[:, 2] + 0.5 * X[:, 3] + rng.normal(0, 0.35, n)   # signal + noise
Xtr, ytr, Xte, yte = X[:180], y[:180], X[180:], y[180:]

big = MLPRegressor(hidden_layer_sizes=(256, 128), max_iter=400, early_stopping=False,
                   learning_rate_init=0.003, random_state=0).fit(Xtr, ytr)
print("no early stopping - train R2:", round(r2_score(ytr, big.predict(Xtr)), 3),
      "  test R2:", round(r2_score(yte, big.predict(Xte)), 3))
print("The gap between those two numbers is overfitting.")

# step: Early stopping - let the validation curve decide when to stop
es = MLPRegressor(hidden_layer_sizes=(256, 128), max_iter=400, early_stopping=True,
                  validation_fraction=0.2, n_iter_no_change=20, learning_rate_init=0.003,
                  random_state=0).fit(Xtr, ytr)
best = int(np.argmax(es.validation_scores_)) + 1
print("stopped at epoch", es.n_iter_, "- best validation score was at epoch", best)
print("early stopping - train R2:", round(r2_score(ytr, es.predict(Xtr)), 3),
      "  test R2:", round(r2_score(yte, es.predict(Xte)), 3))

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(7.5, 3))
ax[0].plot(es.loss_curve_); ax[0].set_title("training loss: always falls"); ax[0].set_xlabel("epoch")
ax[1].plot(es.validation_scores_); ax[1].axvline(best - 1, ls="--", c="k", lw=1)
ax[1].set_title("validation R2: stops when this stalls"); ax[1].set_xlabel("epoch")
plt.tight_layout()

# step: Weight decay - a sweep judged on validation, never on test
for alpha in (1e-4, 1e-2, 1e-1, 1.0):
    m = MLPRegressor(hidden_layer_sizes=(256, 128), alpha=alpha, max_iter=400, early_stopping=True,
                     validation_fraction=0.2, n_iter_no_change=20, learning_rate_init=0.003,
                     random_state=0).fit(Xtr, ytr)
    print(f"alpha {alpha:<7} best validation R2 {max(m.validation_scores_):6.3f}   stopped at epoch {m.n_iter_:3d}")
print("\nChoose alpha from this column. The test rows have not been looked at.")

# step: Leakage - why a random split flatters sensor data
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)             # fresh generator so this step stands alone
def slow_signal(n, phi=0.95):              # a value that drifts slowly, like a room temperature
    x = np.zeros(n)
    for t in range(1, n):
        x[t] = phi * x[t-1] + rng.normal()
    return x

N = 2000                                   # imagine 2000 ten-minute readings
S = np.column_stack([slow_signal(N) for _ in range(4)])       # four slow "sensors"
drift = np.cumsum(rng.normal(size=N)) * 0.3                   # something unmeasured, drifting
t = np.sin(S[:, 0]) + 0.5 * S[:, 1] * S[:, 2] + drift + rng.normal(0, 0.3, N)
print("correlation between reading t and reading t+1:", round(np.corrcoef(t[:-1], t[1:])[0, 1], 3))

from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
knn = KNeighborsRegressor(5)
Str, Ste, ttr, tte = train_test_split(S, t, test_size=0.2, random_state=0)      # random split
knn.fit(Str, ttr); r_random = r2_score(tte, knn.predict(Ste))
cut = int(0.8 * N)                                                             # chronological
knn.fit(S[:cut], t[:cut]); r_chrono = r2_score(t[cut:], knn.predict(S[cut:]))
print(f"k-nearest neighbours   random split R2 = {r_random:6.3f}   chronological R2 = {r_chrono:6.3f}")
print("Same data, same model. The random split let each test row's neighbours in time into training.")
:::

:::keyidea
A negative R² means the model is worse than predicting the mean. It is not a bug in the
lab. It is what a model that learned "look at the neighbouring rows" does when the
neighbouring rows are no longer available.
:::

## Split by the question you are asking

:::narration
The split is not a technicality; it is the question you are asking the model. A
random split asks, can you predict rows that look like ones you trained on? For
sensor data, where every reading resembles its neighbour, that question is nearly
trivial. A chronological split asks, will this work next month? A split grouped by
building asks, will this work on a building you have never seen? Those are different
questions with different answers, and a paper that reports one while implying another
has made a claim it cannot support. Decide which question you are asking, then split
so that the test set can answer it.
:::

:::figure{id=split_strategies caption="Figure 3. Three splits, three different questions. Redrawn for this module."}
:::

:::compare
Random split | shuffle, hold out 20% | Answers: can the model predict rows like the ones it trained on? For time-series or repeated-geometry data, usually a leak.
Grouped split | by building, by geometry, by time block | Answers: will it work on something new? Lower scores, honest ones. Report the grouping with the number.
:::

:::keyidea
Random train/test splitting can overestimate performance when neighbouring
buildings, repeated simulations, time-adjacent samples, or nearly identical
geometries appear in both sets. Episode 3.7 measures exactly what that costs on real
ten-minute sensor data: **R² 0.380 on a random split, and the score collapses on a
chronological one.**
:::

## Evaluation must match the task

:::narration
One score is never enough, and the right scores depend on the task. For regression,
report the error in the units of the decision, the R squared, and where the errors
fall: which building types, which climates. For classification, the confusion matrix
and what each kind of mistake costs. For segmentation, overlap measures like
intersection over union. For physical fields, pointwise error is not enough; report
whether the boundaries and the conservation laws are respected, and how the model
behaves at the extremes. And for generated designs, the question is whether the
constraints are satisfied and whether an expert would use it, which no single number
captures.
:::

- **Regression** — MAE, RMSE, R², residual patterns, and errors across building types
  or climates.
- **Classification** — confusion matrix, accuracy, precision, recall, F1, and the
  consequences of false positives and false negatives.
- **Image segmentation** — intersection over union, Dice score, boundary quality, and
  failures under different image conditions.
- **Physical-field prediction** — pointwise error, field-pattern accuracy, boundary
  adherence, conservation or physical residual, and extreme-condition performance.
- **Design generation** — constraint satisfaction, diversity, feasibility,
  performance, expert review, and human usefulness.

## Architecture-specific generalization tests

:::narration
A single held-out score answers the easiest possible question. The tests that matter
are the ones that resemble how the model will actually be used: new buildings from the
same dataset, then a building type that was absent from training, then a different
city or climate, a different scale, extreme conditions, and noisy or incomplete
sensors. You will not pass all of them, and you are not expected to. You are expected
to report which ones you ran and what happened, because that is the range of the
claim you are entitled to make.
:::

Report a single held-out score and you have answered the easiest possible question.
These are the ones that matter in practice:

- New buildings from the same dataset.
- A building type absent from training.
- A different city or climate zone.
- A different geometric scale or topology.
- Extreme weather or operating conditions.
- Noisy or incomplete sensor data.

:::boundary
Data | the lab's datasets are synthetic and generated by known rules; the R² 0.380 figure is [[measured]] on the Episode 3.7 house
Split | the lab shows random vs chronological on the same rows; grouped-by-building splits are described, not run
Evidence | every lab number is [[simulated]] and will reproduce exactly with the seeds shown
Establishes | that training scores are not results, that the split defines the question, and that early stopping and weight decay are cheap
Does not establish | which regulariser is best for your data — that is a validation-set decision, every time
:::

:::reflect
Your model scores R² 0.91 on a held-out 20% of a simulation dataset. What should you
check first?
- Nothing — 0.91 is a strong result
- *Whether repeated runs of the same geometry landed in both the training and test sets
- Whether a deeper network would score higher
:::
