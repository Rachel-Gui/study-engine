## Central question

How does a neural network move from raw pixels to architectural features and
decisions?

:::narration
An M L P treats every input as an independent number, which is fine for sensor
readings and wrong for an image. A pixel means something because of the pixels around
it. A convolutional network builds that assumption in: it slides small filters across
the image, so a filter that has learned to find an edge finds edges everywhere,
rather than having to learn edge-finding separately for every position. That single
idea, weight sharing across position, is what makes vision tractable. In this
episode you will apply a filter by hand, count the parameters it saves, and then look
hard at what a facade classifier can and cannot claim.
:::

:::flow
Pixels > Convolution: local patterns > Pooling: less resolution, more meaning > Deeper layers: parts and objects > Head: the answer you asked for
:::

By the end of this episode you should be able to:

- Compute one convolution output by hand and say what the kernel detects
- Explain weight sharing and why it makes image models feasible
- Read a CNN diagram: what shrinks, what grows, and why
- State the claim boundary of a facade classifier

## Convolution: one filter, every position

:::narration
A convolution is a small grid of weights, typically three by three, called a kernel.
Place it over a patch of the image, multiply each pixel by the weight underneath,
sum, and write that one number into the output. Then slide one pixel over and do it
again. The output is a feature map: a picture of where in the image that pattern
occurs. A kernel with minus one on the left and plus one on the right responds
wherever a dark column sits beside a bright one, which is to say, at vertical edges.
The network learns the nine numbers. It does not learn them separately at each
position; the same nine are used everywhere. Edit them in the widget and watch the
feature map change.
:::

:::figure{id=convolution caption="Figure 1. A 3×3 kernel slides over a 6×6 image; the 4×4 feature map records where the pattern occurs; pooling keeps the strongest response in each block. Redrawn for this module."}
:::

:::widget{id=conv-lab}
:::

:::keyidea
**Weight sharing.** A dense layer needs one weight per input per output. A convolution
needs nine weights, used at every position. The saving is not a detail: for a
224×224 image it is the difference between thousands of parameters and billions.
:::

:::predict
A kernel has −1 in its top row, 0 in the middle row, and +1 in the bottom row. On the
facade in the widget, where will it respond most strongly?
- Along the left and right sides of each window | Those are vertical edges. This kernel compares rows, not columns.
- *Along the top and bottom edges of each window | Yes. It compares the row above with the row below, so it fires where brightness changes vertically.
- Uniformly across the wall | A flat wall has no change in any direction, so the sum is zero everywhere.
:::

## CNN workflow

:::narration
A full network stacks this idea. Early layers run many kernels over the image and
produce many feature maps: edges, corners, textures. Pooling halves the resolution
and keeps the strongest response in each block, so the next layer sees a coarser,
more abstract picture. Deeper layers combine early features into parts: a window, a
balcony, a course of brick. By the end, the spatial grid is tiny and the number of
channels is large, and a final head turns that into whatever you asked for: a class, a
bounding box, a segmentation mask, a number. Notice the shape of the diagram. Width
and height shrink; depth grows. That is meaning replacing position.
:::

1. Input an image, map, voxel grid, or simulation raster.
2. Convolution filters detect local patterns.
3. Feature maps encode edges, textures, shapes, and higher-level structures.
4. {{Activation function}}s introduce nonlinearity.
5. Pooling or downsampling reduces spatial resolution and compresses information.
6. Deeper layers combine local features into abstract representations.
7. The output head produces a class, bounding box, segmentation mask, numerical
   prediction, or generated image.

:::figure{id=cnn_stack caption="Figure 2. Spatial size shrinks, channels grow, the head answers the question. Redrawn for this module."}
:::

:::cards
Classification | one label per image | facade material: brick, render, glass
Detection | boxes around things | windows, doors, defects, signs
Segmentation | one label per pixel | roof, wall, glazing, vegetation from aerial imagery
Regression on rasters | a number per grid cell | solar, wind, and thermal fields on a grid
:::

:::technical{summary="Parameter counts, receptive fields, and the transfer-learning shortcut"}
A convolution layer with k×k kernels, C_in input channels and C_out output channels
has k·k·C_in·C_out + C_out parameters, regardless of the image size. A 3×3 layer from
64 to 128 channels: 73,856. A dense layer from a 56×56×64 tensor to 256 units:
51,380,480.

The **receptive field** of a unit is the patch of the input that can influence it.
Each 3×3 layer adds two pixels; each 2× pooling doubles the growth. By the end of a
typical network one unit sees the whole facade.

In practice nobody trains a facade classifier from scratch. You take a network
pretrained on millions of general photographs (ResNet, EfficientNet, ConvNeXt), keep
its early layers, and retrain only the head on your few thousand labelled facades.
This is **transfer learning**, and it is why architectural vision projects are
feasible with small datasets.
:::

:::flow
Street-view facade images > image quality control and labels > CNN feature extractor > material classification > confidence and confusion matrix > GIS aggregation > human verification
:::

## Interactive code lab — convolution and pooling in numpy

:::narration
The lab draws a facade as a twenty-four by twenty-four grid of numbers, writes a
convolution function in six lines, and runs a vertical and a horizontal edge kernel
over it. You will see two feature maps that pick out different parts of the same
windows. Then max pooling: the map shrinks to a quarter of the cells and the windows
are still there. The last step counts parameters. A convolution layer producing that
feature map uses ten. A dense layer producing the same map would use about two
hundred and eighty thousand, and would have to learn what an edge is four hundred and
eighty four separate times.
:::

:::pylab{title="Convolution and pooling by hand" packages=numpy,matplotlib}
# step: Draw a facade as a grid of numbers
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

img = np.full((24, 24), 0.15)                  # a plain wall
img += 0.04 * ((np.arange(24)[:, None] + np.arange(24)[None, :]) % 3 == 0)   # faint brick texture
for r0 in (3, 10, 17):                          # three rows of windows
    for c0 in (2, 9, 16):
        img[r0:r0 + 4, c0:c0 + 5] = 0.9
img[19:24, 10:14] = 0.7                         # a door
print("image shape:", img.shape, " values from", img.min(), "to", img.max())

plt.figure(figsize=(3, 3)); plt.imshow(img, cmap="gray"); plt.axis("off")
_ = plt.title("input")

# step: Convolution - one kernel, every position
def convolve(image, kernel):
    """Valid convolution (no padding). Output is smaller by kernel size - 1."""
    kh, kw = kernel.shape
    H, W = image.shape[0] - kh + 1, image.shape[1] - kw + 1
    out = np.zeros((H, W))
    for i in range(H):
        for j in range(W):
            out[i, j] = np.sum(image[i:i + kh, j:j + kw] * kernel)
    return out

vertical   = np.array([[-1, 0, 1], [-1, 0, 1], [-1, 0, 1]])     # responds to left/right edges
horizontal = vertical.T                                        # responds to top/bottom edges

fv = np.maximum(0, convolve(img, vertical))      # ReLU: keep the positive responses
fh = np.maximum(0, convolve(img, horizontal))
print("feature map shape:", fv.shape)
print("cells that fire (vertical kernel):", int((fv > 0.5).sum()), " (horizontal):", int((fh > 0.5).sum()))

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(8, 2.8))
for a, m, t in zip(ax, [img, fv, fh], ["input", "vertical edges", "horizontal edges"]):
    a.imshow(m, cmap="gray"); a.set_title(t); a.axis("off")
plt.tight_layout()

# step: Pooling - keep the strongest response in each 2x2 block
def max_pool(m, k=2):
    H, W = m.shape[0] // k, m.shape[1] // k
    return m[:H * k, :W * k].reshape(H, k, W, k).max(axis=(1, 3))

pv = max_pool(fv)
print("pooled shape:", pv.shape, " - a quarter of the cells, the windows still visible")
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(5.5, 2.8))
ax[0].imshow(fv, cmap="gray"); ax[0].set_title("22x22 feature map"); ax[0].axis("off")
ax[1].imshow(pv, cmap="gray"); ax[1].set_title("11x11 after max-pool"); ax[1].axis("off")
plt.tight_layout()

# step: Why weight sharing matters - count the parameters
pixels = img.size                       # 576
conv_params = 3 * 3 + 1                 # nine weights and a bias, used at every position
dense_params = pixels * fv.size + fv.size   # a dense layer producing the same 22x22 map
print(f"convolution layer : {conv_params:>8,} parameters")
print(f"dense layer       : {dense_params:>8,} parameters for the same output")
print(f"ratio             : {dense_params // conv_params:,}x")
print("\nThe dense layer would also have to learn 'edge' separately at every one of the 484 positions.")
:::

## Architectural applications

:::narration
Where this is used. Facade material classification from street view, at city scale.
Detection of windows, doors, roofs and defects. Progress and safety monitoring on
construction sites. Aerial and satellite analysis of urban form. Accessibility and
visual-quality assessment. And, less obviously, surrogate models on grids: a solar
map, a wind field, a thermal field is a raster, and a convolutional network can learn
to predict one from geometry far faster than the simulator that produced the
training data. That last use is where Episode three point six picks up.
:::

- Facade material classification.
- Window, door, roof, and building-feature detection.
- Construction progress, defect, and safety monitoring.
- Street-view, aerial-image, and satellite urban analysis.
- Accessibility and visual-design evaluation.
- Grid-based solar, wind, thermal, and pollution-field surrogate modeling.

:::compare
MLP on pixels | every pixel is an independent input | No notion of neighbourhood; a window in the corner and a window in the centre are unrelated problems. Millions of weights, poor generalisation.
CNN | kernels shared across position | Neighbourhood built in; a pattern learned once is found anywhere. Far fewer weights, and it is what every vision model uses.
:::

## Risks and limitations

:::narration
Now the boundary, and it is a serious one. A facade classifier reports a surface
appearance. It does not see the wall assembly behind it, its U value, or its
condition. Cameras, lighting, seasons and occlusion all shift the pixels, and a model
trained on one city's imagery can fail quietly in another. Models also learn
shortcuts: if brick facades in the training set tend to have trees in front of them,
the model may be detecting trees. And the leakage lesson from the last episode
applies with force. If photographs of the same building are in both training and
test sets, your score measures recognition of that building, not generalisation to a
new one. Split by building.
:::

- Geographic and visual-data bias.
- Differences in cameras, lighting, occlusion, seasons, and image quality.
- The model may learn background context instead of the intended architectural
  feature.
- Visible material does not reveal the complete wall assembly or performance.
- Grid-based CNNs can struggle with irregular geometry and very high-resolution 3D
  domains.

:::keyidea
A facade classifier that reports "brick" has recognised a surface appearance. It has
not established the wall assembly, its U-value, or its condition. Treat the output as
a triage signal that routes a building to a human, not as a survey result.
:::

:::keyidea
If photographs of the same facade appear in both training and test sets, the test
score measures recognition of that facade rather than {{generalization}} to a new
building. Split by building, not by image.
:::

:::boundary
Data | the lab image is a synthetic 24×24 grid; no photographs were classified in this episode
Evidence | lab outputs are [[simulated]]; application claims describe published practice, not results measured here
Establishes | what a convolution computes, why weight sharing works, what pooling discards, how a CNN is shaped
Does not establish | that any facade classifier is accurate on your city, your camera, or your building stock — that is a per-dataset test, split by building
:::

:::reflect
A model classifies facade material from street-view images with 94% accuracy on a
random image-level split. The images come from 600 buildings, 8 photos each. What is
the honest next step?
- Deploy it; 94% is high | The split almost certainly put the same building on both sides. The number is not yet meaningful.
- *Re-split by building so no building appears in both sets, and re-measure | Yes. Expect a lower number. That lower number is the result.
- Add more layers to push it past 95% | Capacity is not the problem. The evaluation is.
:::
