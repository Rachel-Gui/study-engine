## Central question

Can a model give fast simulation feedback while staying physically plausible under
conditions it was never trained on?

:::narration
This episode is mostly about vocabulary, and that is not a criticism. Five terms get
used interchangeably in papers and job adverts, and they mean genuinely different
things. Physics-informed machine learning is an umbrella. A P I N N is one specific
method under it. A surrogate model is defined by what it replaces, not by how it is
built. And an implicit neural representation is a way of storing a field, which is
not physics-informed unless you explicitly make it so. Getting these apart is most of
the work. The rest of the work is watching each one fail in its characteristic way,
which the two widgets in this episode let you do in a few clicks.
:::

:::flow
Simulation: exact, slow > Surrogate: fast, valid in a range > PINN: the equation in the loss > INR: a field as a function > Validate all of them
:::

By the end of this episode you should be able to:

- Place a method on the spectrum from physics-based to purely data-driven
- Say what a PINN's loss contains and what each term buys
- Explain why a surrogate's sampled range is part of its deliverable
- Tell an INR from a PINN and say when one becomes the other

## The modeling spectrum

:::narration
Put every model on one line. At one end, physics-based simulation: governing
equations, boundary conditions, a solver. It is explicit and trustworthy and slow.
At the other end, purely data-driven: learn from examples, fast once trained, and
free to violate physics if the data allows it. In between sit hybrid or grey-box
models, where the mechanisms you know are written down and the ones you do not are
learned. Physics-informed machine learning is the umbrella over all the ways of
moving a data-driven model leftward: physics as features, as architecture, as loss
terms, as bounds. Where a method sits on this line tells you what it can promise.
:::

:::cards
Physics-based simulation | Governing equations, boundary conditions, solvers | Explicit physical foundation, high cost
Purely data-driven | Learns from measured or simulated examples | Fast after training, may violate physics
Hybrid / gray-box | Simplified physics plus learned components | For when some mechanisms are known
Physics-informed ML | The umbrella term, not one method | Physics enters many possible ways
:::

**Physics-informed machine learning** is an umbrella term for introducing physical
knowledge into machine learning. Physics may enter as inputs, features, architecture,
loss terms, bounds, monotonic relationships, conservation rules, or model ensembles.

:::cards
As features | degree-days, solar angles, U-values | the cheapest way in; no new method needed
As architecture | symmetries, conservation built into layers | the model cannot produce certain wrong answers
As a loss term | equation residuals, boundary conditions | the PINN route; physics is a penalty, not a guarantee
As bounds | monotonic and range constraints | more glass never reduces solar gain, and the model knows it
:::

**{{PINN}}** — a specific neural-network method within physics-informed learning. It
usually adds governing-equation residuals and boundary or initial conditions to the
loss, and can learn from both observations and physical constraints. It is not
automatically superior; complex boundaries, stiff equations, scale, and optimization
can all remain difficult.

**{{Surrogate model}}** — approximates a slower high-fidelity simulator to support
rapid repeated prediction during design exploration, optimization, or control. Its
reliability is limited by the simulation dataset, the parameter range, the geometry
representation, and the validation method.

**{{INR}}** — represents geometry or a physical field as a continuous
coordinate-based neural function. Coordinates plus a learned shape encoding can
produce wind velocity, temperature, pressure, or another field variable. Useful for
complex geometry and flexible-resolution queries.

:::keyidea
Physics-informed ML, PINN, hybrid model, surrogate model and INR are **not
interchangeable terms**. An INR is not automatically physics-informed — physical
constraints have to be added explicitly when they are required.
:::

:::compare
PINN | the equation is in the loss | Learns from physics with little or no data; struggles with stiff equations and complex boundaries; one solve per training run.
Surrogate | the simulator is in the training set | Learns from many simulator runs; fast and general inside the sampled range, unreliable outside it.
:::

## PINN: the equation becomes a loss term

:::narration
Here is what a physics-informed neural network actually is, and it is less exotic than
the name. Take an ordinary network whose input is a coordinate, x and t, and whose
output is the field value at that point, temperature say. Because the network is a
differentiable function, automatic differentiation can give you its derivatives with
respect to its inputs, exactly. So you can evaluate the governing equation at any
point and measure how badly the network violates it. That violation, the residual,
becomes a loss term. Add a term for the boundary conditions and a term for any
measurements you have, weight them, and train. The physics is a penalty. It is
minimised, not satisfied, and the widget below shows what that difference costs.
:::

:::figure{id=pinn_loss caption="Figure 1. A PINN is a normal network with an unusual loss: data, equation residual, and boundary terms, weighted and summed. Redrawn for this module."}
:::

:::keyidea
L = λ₁ · data loss + λ₂ · PDE residual + λ₃ · boundary loss. The residual is measured
at **collocation points** — places where the equation is checked, not places where the
answer is known. Between and beyond them, nothing is enforced.
:::

:::widget{id=pinn-lab}
:::

:::predict
You train the widget's PINN with the boundary term switched off (λ boundary = 0).
The PDE residual reaches nearly zero. Is the solution right?
- Yes — zero residual means the equation is satisfied | Satisfied, but the equation alone has infinitely many solutions. Which one you got is an accident of initialisation.
- *No — without boundary conditions the equation does not pin down a unique solution | Yes. u = sin(πx) + a + bx solves u″ = −π²sin(πx) for any a and b. The boundary term is what selects one.
- Only if the network is large enough | Size is irrelevant. The problem is under-determined, not under-fitted.
:::

:::technical{summary="Why PINNs are hard in practice — and what people do about it"}
The three loss terms have different scales and different gradients. The residual of a
second-order equation involves second derivatives of the network and is typically
orders of magnitude larger than the boundary error, so the boundary gets ignored
unless λ₃ is large — which is why the widget defaults to λ₃ = 10. Practical remedies:

- **Loss balancing** — adapt the λs during training so no term dominates (Wang,
  Teng & Perdikaris, 2021).
- **Hard constraints** — build the boundary condition into the architecture, e.g.
  u(x) = x(1−x)·NN(x) is zero at both ends by construction, so λ₃ disappears.
- **Collocation sampling** — resample collocation points where the residual is largest.
- **Stiff or multiscale equations** remain difficult: a tanh network struggles to
  represent sharp fronts, and gradient descent on the residual can stall in flat
  regions. A PINN is not a replacement for a solver on those problems; it is a
  different tool with a different failure profile.

Episode 3.9 builds one from scratch, with the derivatives written by hand so every
number can be checked.
:::

## Surrogate models and the sampled range

:::narration
A surrogate is a fast stand-in for a slow simulator. You run the real simulator a few
hundred times across the designs you are considering, train a model on those runs,
and then query the model thousands of times inside a design loop. It works, and it is
probably the most-used idea in this episode. The catch is the phrase "designs you
are considering". The surrogate has seen a box in parameter space. Inside the box it
is interpolating between real simulations, and its error can be measured. Outside the
box it is extrapolating with a smooth function, and the number it returns looks
exactly as confident. Drag the query in the widget past the shaded band and watch.
:::

:::figure{id=surrogate_pipeline caption="Figure 2. Simulator, dataset, surrogate, design loop — and the check that closes the loop. Redrawn for this module."}
:::

:::widget{id=surrogate-lab}
:::

:::figure{id=interpolation_region caption="Figure 3. Inside the cloud of training runs the surrogate interpolates; outside it guesses. Redrawn for this module."}
:::

:::keyidea
A fast surrogate does not always replace high-fidelity simulation. It replaces it
*inside the range it was fitted on*, and nowhere else. Marking where that range ends
is part of the deliverable.
:::

## Interactive code lab — a surrogate, inside and outside its box

:::narration
The lab plays the design team. A function stands in for a slow simulator. The team
runs it eighty times, all within a window-to-wall ratio of point two to point six and
orientations from east through south to west. A Gaussian process is trained as the
surrogate; on held-out runs inside that box its error is about one kilowatt-hour per
square metre. Then we ask it about designs outside the box, and the error jumps by a
factor of five to twenty. The model's own uncertainty estimate barely moves, because
a Gaussian process only knows what its kernel assumes. The last step draws the error
over the whole parameter plane, with the sampled box outlined. That picture is what
you should hand over with every surrogate.
:::

:::pylab{title="Where a surrogate is valid" packages=numpy,scikit-learn,matplotlib}
# step: A stand-in for a slow simulator
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def simulate(wwr, orient_deg):
    """Pretend this takes an hour per call. Cooling load, kWh/m2, illustrative."""
    solar = 0.5 * (1 - np.cos(np.radians(orient_deg)))      # 0 facing north, 1 facing south
    return 20 + 70 * wwr**2 * (0.4 + 0.6 * solar) + 5 * np.sin(6 * wwr) + rng.normal(0, 0.8)

# the design team ran the simulator 80 times, all in the range they were considering
runs_wwr = rng.uniform(0.2, 0.6, 80)
runs_orient = rng.uniform(90, 270, 80)          # east to west through south
runs_load = np.array([simulate(w, o) for w, o in zip(runs_wwr, runs_orient)])
print("80 simulator runs.  WWR in [0.20, 0.60], orientation in [90, 270] degrees")
print("load range in the runs: %.1f to %.1f kWh/m2" % (runs_load.min(), runs_load.max()))

# step: Fit the surrogate and check it inside the range
from sklearn.gaussian_process import GaussianProcessRegressor
from sklearn.gaussian_process.kernels import RBF, WhiteKernel
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error

Xs = np.column_stack([runs_wwr, runs_orient])
sc = StandardScaler().fit(Xs)
y_mean = runs_load[:64].mean()
# a Gaussian process: a common surrogate, and it reports its own uncertainty
surrogate = GaussianProcessRegressor(kernel=RBF(1.0) + WhiteKernel(0.1), random_state=0,
                                     normalize_y=False).fit(sc.transform(Xs[:64]), runs_load[:64] - y_mean)

pred = surrogate.predict(sc.transform(Xs[64:])) + y_mean
inside = mean_absolute_error(runs_load[64:], pred)
print("16 held-out runs inside the range: MAE = %.2f kWh/m2" % inside)
print("That is the number you may report - with the range attached.")

# step: Now ask it about designs outside the range
queries = [(0.40, 180, "inside"), (0.80, 180, "WWR outside"), (0.40, 0, "orientation outside"),
           (0.90, 20, "both outside")]
print(f"{'design':22s} {'surrogate':>10s} {'simulator':>10s} {'error':>8s} {'its own +/-':>12s}")
for w, o, label in queries:
    p, sd = surrogate.predict(sc.transform([[w, o]]), return_std=True)
    t = simulate(w, o)
    print(f"{label:22s} {p[0] + y_mean:10.1f} {t:10.1f} {p[0] + y_mean - t:+8.1f} {sd[0]:12.1f}")
print("\nThe error outside the box is 5 to 20 times the error inside it. The model's own +/- barely moves:")
print("a Gaussian process only knows what its kernel assumes. A neural surrogate would not even have")
print("that column. The sampled box is the deliverable, not any number the model prints.")

# step: Draw the range on the map
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
W, O = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(0, 1, 60), np.linspace(0, 360, 60))
grid = np.column_stack([W.ravel(), O.ravel()])
err = np.abs(surrogate.predict(sc.transform(grid)) + y_mean - np.array([simulate(w, o) for w, o in grid]))
plt.figure(figsize=(6, 3.6))
plt.contourf(W, O, err.reshape(W.shape), levels=[0, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 200], cmap="Purples")
plt.colorbar(label="|surrogate - simulator|, kWh/m2")
plt.scatter(runs_wwr, runs_orient, s=8, c="k", label="simulator runs")
plt.gca().add_patch(plt.Rectangle((0.2, 90), 0.4, 180, fill=False, ls="--", lw=1.5, ec="k"))
plt.xlabel("window-to-wall ratio"); plt.ylabel("orientation (deg)"); plt.legend(loc="upper left")
plt.title("Error is small inside the sampled box and grows outside it")
plt.tight_layout()
:::

## Implicit neural representations

:::narration
An implicit neural representation stores a field as a function rather than a grid.
Feed a network a coordinate, x y z, and it returns the value there: a temperature, a
pressure, a signed distance to the nearest surface. There is no mesh; you can query
any point at any resolution. Two things make this work in practice. First, plain
networks are biased towards smooth outputs, so INRs use sine activations or Fourier
features to represent fine detail. Second, a learned shape code lets one network
represent many geometries. What an INR is not is physics-informed. It is storage. If
you want the field it stores to obey an equation, you add that equation to its loss,
and at that moment it becomes a PINN.
:::

:::figure{id=inr caption="Figure 4. Coordinates in, value out: a field stored as a function, with no mesh. Redrawn for this module."}
:::

:::compare
Grid or mesh | values stored at fixed cells | Resolution fixed at creation; memory grows with the cube of resolution; interpolation between cells is your problem.
INR | values stored as a network | Query anywhere; memory is the weight count; fine detail needs sine or Fourier features; differentiable, which is how it becomes a PINN.
:::

:::technical{summary="SIREN, Fourier features, and the spectral bias"}
A ReLU network trained on coordinates learns low frequencies first and fine detail
slowly or never — the **spectral bias** (Rahaman et al., 2019). Two fixes are
standard: map each coordinate through a bank of sines and cosines at increasing
frequencies before the first layer (**Fourier features**, Tancik et al., 2020), or use
sin(·) as the activation throughout (**SIREN**, Sitzmann et al., 2020). Both let a
small network represent a wind field with sharp gradients around a building corner.
Because a SIREN is smooth and its derivatives are also SIRENs, it is a natural
backbone for a PINN: the same network can be asked for ∂u/∂x exactly.
:::

## Two worked pipelines

:::narration
Two pipelines you will meet in practice. The first is a control loop: weather, H V A
C state, occupancy and the current indoor temperature go into a model that carries
physical constraints, and it predicts the next indoor temperature, which drives a
comfort and energy decision. The second is design exploration: building geometry is
encoded, a neural surrogate or a neural field predicts a wind or thermal field, and
comfort and facade-pressure metrics come out fast enough to iterate on the massing
in a design session. In both, the model is inside a loop that acts on its output,
which is exactly why its validity range has to be known.
:::

:::flow
Weather > HVAC control > occupancy > current indoor temperature > data model plus physical constraints > next-step indoor temperature > comfort and energy-control decision
:::

:::flow
Building geometry > geometry encoder or latent representation > neural surrogate or neural field > wind or thermal field > pedestrian-comfort and facade-pressure metrics > rapid design iteration
:::

## Validation requirements

:::narration
How to validate any of these. Compare against an independent simulation or a
measurement, never against the training runs. Test across geometries, scales,
climates and boundary conditions, not just held-out samples from the same box.
Report the field error, the error at the extremes, the physical residual, and the
uncertainty. Mark the interpolation region and the extrapolation region explicitly.
And for critical decisions keep the high-fidelity simulator or an expert in the loop,
because a surrogate that is fast and wrong is worse than a simulator that is slow.
:::

- Compare against independent simulation or measurement.
- Test different geometries, scales, climates, and boundary conditions.
- Report field error, extreme-value error, physical residual, and {{uncertainty}}.
- Mark interpolation and extrapolation regions.
- Keep high-fidelity simulation or expert review in the loop for critical decisions.

:::boundary
Data | the lab's simulator is a synthetic function; the widget's PINN solves a one-dimensional textbook equation
Evidence | every number in this episode is [[simulated]]; no measured field was reproduced
Establishes | what each term of a PINN loss does, why a surrogate's range is part of its output, what an INR stores
Does not establish | that a PINN or surrogate matches a real CFD or energy simulation on your geometry — that is a validation study with its own held-out runs
:::

:::reflect
You train a network on coordinates to reproduce a wind field. Is it physics-informed?
- Yes — it represents a physical field
- *Not unless you added physical constraints such as equation residuals to the loss
- Yes, because it replaces a CFD solver
:::
