## Central question

Can a model give fast simulation feedback while staying physically plausible under
conditions it was never trained on?

:::narration
This episode is mostly about vocabulary, and that is not a criticism. Five terms get
used interchangeably in papers and job adverts, and they mean genuinely different
things. Physics-informed machine learning is an umbrella. A P I N N is one specific
method under it. A surrogate model is defined by what it replaces, not by how it is
built. And an implicit neural representation is a way of storing a field, which is
not physics-informed unless you explicitly make it so. Getting these apart is most of
the work.
:::

## The modeling spectrum

:::cards
Physics-based simulation | Governing equations, boundary conditions, solvers | Explicit physical foundation, high cost
Purely data-driven | Learns from measured or simulated examples | Fast after training, may violate physics
Hybrid / gray-box | Simplified physics plus learned components | For when some mechanisms are known
Physics-informed ML | The umbrella term, not one method | Physics enters many possible ways
:::

**Physics-informed machine learning** is an umbrella term for introducing physical
knowledge into machine learning. Physics may enter as inputs, features, architecture,
loss terms, bounds, monotonic relationships, conservation rules, or model ensembles.

**{{PINN}}** — a specific neural-network method within physics-informed learning. It
usually adds governing-equation residuals and boundary or initial conditions to the
loss, and can learn from both observations and physical constraints. It is not
automatically superior; complex boundaries, stiff equations, scale, and optimization
can all remain difficult.

**{{Surrogate model}}** — approximates a slower high-fidelity simulator to support
rapid repeated prediction during design exploration, optimization, or control. Its
reliability is limited by the simulation dataset, the parameter range, the geometry
representation, and the validation method.

**{{INR}}** — represents geometry or a physical field as a continuous
coordinate-based neural function. Coordinates plus a learned shape encoding can
produce wind velocity, temperature, pressure, or another field variable. Useful for
complex geometry and flexible-resolution queries.

:::keyidea
Physics-informed ML, PINN, hybrid model, surrogate model and INR are **not
interchangeable terms**. An INR is not automatically physics-informed — physical
constraints have to be added explicitly when they are required.
:::

## Two worked pipelines

:::flow
Weather > HVAC control > occupancy > current indoor temperature > data model plus physical constraints > next-step indoor temperature > comfort and energy-control decision
:::

:::flow
Building geometry > geometry encoder or latent representation > neural surrogate or neural field > wind or thermal field > pedestrian-comfort and facade-pressure metrics > rapid design iteration
:::

## Validation requirements

- Compare against independent simulation or measurement.
- Test different geometries, scales, climates, and boundary conditions.
- Report field error, extreme-value error, physical residual, and {{uncertainty}}.
- Mark interpolation and extrapolation regions.
- Keep high-fidelity simulation or expert review in the loop for critical decisions.

:::keyidea
A fast surrogate does not always replace high-fidelity simulation. It replaces it
*inside the range it was fitted on*, and nowhere else. Marking where that range ends
is part of the deliverable.
:::

:::reflect
You train a network on coordinates to reproduce a wind field. Is it physics-informed?
- Yes — it represents a physical field
- *Not unless you added physical constraints such as equation residuals to the loss
- Yes, because it replaces a CFD solver
:::
